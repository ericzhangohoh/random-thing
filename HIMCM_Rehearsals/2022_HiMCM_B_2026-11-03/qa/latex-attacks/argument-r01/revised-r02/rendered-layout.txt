                         Carbon Dioxide and Temperature
                                   Evidence, Extrapolation and Limits


The supplied records support a comparison of forecast rules, but they do not turn a century-scale ex-
trapolation into a reliable prediction. We examine annual Mauna Loa CO2 concentrations and global
temperature anomalies through 2021, beginning with the definitions behind the problem’s claims. The
ten-year rise ending in 2004 is 18.74 ppm, slightly below the 18.77 ppm rise ending in 2003. This small
arithmetic difference has no established physical significance, and the annual data cannot settle the sep-
arate March-specific assertion.
   Three concentration rules represent recent absolute growth (D), constant acceleration (Q) and pro-
portional growth (E). D has the lowest pooled development error. On the later 2012–2021 assessment,
however, Q is more accurate, and rankings also vary across development origins. We retain D as the
choice under the prespecified rule without treating it as a universal future winner.

None reaches 685 ppm by 2050. The OECD figure cited in the problem describes greenhouse gases in
CO2 -equivalent, so this pure-CO2 exercise does not test the same quantity [6].
   Using the selected concentration rule in the temperature relationship gives T1-D, which reaches
anomalies of 1.25, 1.50 and 2.00◦ C above the 1951–1980 mean in 2036, 2048 and 2076. It improves on
the specified full-history linear-time baseline, although the declared T1-Q comparator has slightly lower
errors. These are first crossing dates on a conditional annual curve, not dates of realized or sustained
warming or Paris-target crossings. Other model forms and fitting windows move them.
  Concentration and temperature are strongly associated in their observed levels, but the association
weakens after trend removal and differencing. These two records do not isolate causation.
   The tests extend only ten years ahead. A range based on earlier temperature errors misses the 2016
observation. Separately, 2031 is the display cutoff obtained by extending the tested ten-year horizon
from 2021. Neither that envelope nor the spread between models supplies probability bounds for dis-
tant projections. Continued measurement, repeated error assessment and comparison of assumptions are
warranted. Estimating an intervention’s effect requires additional evidence on emissions, forcing and
response dynamics.

1     Data, definitions and scope

The modeling problem contains three different questions: what the historical records say, how alterna-
tive mathematical rules extend those records, and what can reasonably be inferred from the extensions.
Keeping these questions separate is essential. A curve can fit past observations well while still giving a
fragile answer far beyond them. Our analysis therefore checks the supplied claims, compares forecasts
made from earlier dates, and carries the observed failures into the conclusions [1].
   The supplied workbook is the sole numerical modeling input. It contains 63 annual CO2 observations
for 1959–2021 and 64 temperature anomalies for 1958–2021. Admission checks found no missing years,
duplicate years, missing values or nonnumeric observations. Matching the two sheets by calendar year
gives 63 paired observations for 1959–2021. The temperature value for 1958 remains in the source record
but is outside the joint modeling sample [2].
   These retrospective tests truncate one supplied data vintage by observation year. They do not recon-
struct the data releases actually available at each historical forecast origin.

                               Table 1. Definitions of the two supplied series.

 Temperature          Global land-ocean anomaly in ◦ C relative to 1951–1980          1958–2021


   These measurements do not have identical spatial meanings. Mauna Loa is a station indicator, whereas
the temperature series combines land-air and sea-surface anomalies into a global index. Negative tem-
perature anomalies are valid observations below the reference mean. All temperature thresholds in this
report use the stated 1951–1980 baseline, with no conversion to pre-industrial warming [3, 5].


1.1    Three source distinctions

The problem describes March concentrations, but its source link is an annual-mean file and the supplied
values follow that series. We therefore interpret the modeling observations as annual means. They can
answer the annual ten-year comparison supported by their coverage, but they cannot directly verify a
March-specific record or a claim about all earlier history [1, 3].
   The May 2022 statement concerns a different frequency. NOAA’s June 2022 release reports monthly
Mauna Loa means of 420.99 ppm from NOAA and 420.78 ppm from Scripps, both consistent with the
rounded figure of 421 ppm. This later observation provides context only and is excluded from fitting and
validation [4].
    There is also a mismatch in the OECD benchmark. Its approximately 685 ppm in 2050 denotes green-
house gases measured as CO2 -equivalent under a conditional baseline. The same source gives separate
CO2 -only levels of approximately 530 ppm in 2050 and 780 ppm in 2100. We retain the requested 685-
ppm pure-CO2 calculation as a mathematical exercise, without calling agreement or disagreement with
it a test of the OECD scenario [6].

3             Concentration models

We use three deliberately simple continuations. They differ in what they hold constant: recent absolute
growth, acceleration, or proportional growth. Let 𝑥 = (𝑡 −1959)/10 and let the reference concentration be
315.98 ppm. One unit of 𝑥 is a decade. Least-squares fits use a stable singular-value-decomposition so-
lution, with full stored precision retained for forecasts and threshold searches. The coefficients displayed
below are rounded.

3.3   Proportional growth: E

The exponential model fits the logarithm of concentration relative to 315.98 ppm as a linear function of

sented by an anomaly relative to 1951–1980 throughout. A full-history linear trend in time, T0, provides
a baseline against which to compare the descriptive log-concentration relationship T1. Fitting through
2021 gives


                            𝑇b0 (𝑡) = −0.170257937 + 0.168183564𝑥,                                               (7)
                          𝑇b1 (𝐶) = −0.100573219 + 3.776119820 log(𝐶/315.98).                                    (8)

The log form associates equal proportional concentration changes with equal changes in fitted anomaly.
It offers a compact way to link the two series, but its slope is a descriptive coefficient rather than an

9     Model appraisal

The framework’s main strength is that its assumptions are visible. Each concentration model has few
parameters, historical fits use only workbook observations dated no later than their origin, and temper-
ature scores include upstream concentration forecast error. Comparing different forms also reveals an
uncertainty that a single fitted curve would hide. This transparency makes the analysis inspectable, even
where its conclusions are limited.
   The same simplicity restricts the model. D assumes recent growth persists, Q lets curvature dominate
distant behavior, and E describes proportional stock growth without an emissions-and-sinks balance. T1
omits response delays and other drivers. More elaborate curves would add flexibility, but the short record

9.1   Calculation validity

All actual full-history and declared last-30-observation paths are finite, valid and nondecreasing over the
annual search domain 2021–2300. Their reported crossing boundaries satisfy the unrounded comparisons,
and the supplied history does not exceed the requested levels. These checks establish that the calculations
have the stated mathematical status. They do not establish climate credibility through 2300.
   A first-passage search uses only the contiguous valid portion of a path from its origin. A crossing
before a later invalid value is retained with a warning, while invalidity before any crossing blocks a date.
Paths already above the threshold at the forecast origin are distinguished from valid paths that do not
cross it by 2300. A later valid segment cannot bridge an invalid gap. All actual primary paths remain
valid throughout the search domain.


10     Conclusions

The historical claim has a narrow but clear answer. In the supplied annual series, the ten-year increase
ending in 2004 is second to the one ending in 2003. The difference is only 0.03 ppm, and it does not settle
a March-specific assertion. Source definitions remain just as consequential in the future comparison: our
pure-CO2 calculations and OECD’s CO2 -equivalent benchmark describe different quantities.
   For the stated concentration models, the 2100 projections range from 583.70 to 688.33 ppm. None
reaches 685 ppm by 2050. The selected temperature path reaches 1.25, 1.50 and 2.00◦ C above 1951–
1980 in 2036, 2048 and 2076, conditional on its continuation. Those answers are useful as consequences
of explicit assumptions. Their precision should not obscure how readily other assumptions move them.
   The tests explain why that distinction is necessary. D wins the declared pooled development com-
parison, yet Q does better in the later period. T1-Q also slightly outperforms the selected T1-D path,
whose historical error reference misses 2016. Refitting the temperature relationship over a shorter win-
dow moves the requested crossings several years earlier. Taken together, these results support comparison
and reassessment rather than confidence in one distant trajectory.
   Historical testing still contributes evidence. It describes relative errors for specified rules over one- to
ten-year tasks and exposes weaknesses that an in-sample fit would miss. It does not supply a universal
expiry date for the model. Keeping definitions consistent, monitoring errors and comparing alternative
assumptions are therefore practical consequences of this analysis. Quantifying a particular intervention’s
benefit would require emissions, forcing and response evidence beyond these two records.

11 Article for Scientific Today

                             Climate numbers need more than a date

A concentration figure can be accurate yet answer the wrong question. We examined the records supplied
through 2021 for a mathematical modeling exercise, beginning with what each number measures.
   The supplied carbon dioxide values are annual Mauna Loa averages, despite the question’s reference
to March. Their ten-year rise ending in 2004 was 18.74 parts per million, just below 18.77 ppm ending
in 2003. That small difference does not settle the separate March claim. NOAA’s source supports about
421 ppm for the monthly average in May 2022, a later observation that we excluded from our fitted
models [1, 2, 3, 4].
  Three simple extensions of past concentration changes produce roughly 466–497 ppm in 2050 and
584–688 ppm in 2100. None reaches 685 ppm by 2050. This does not refute OECD, whose cited figure
combines greenhouse gases as CO2 -equivalent rather than measuring CO2 alone [6].
   Model choice changes the answer. A recent-growth rule performed best in the planned development
comparison, but a quadratic curve did better on the later 2012–2021 assessment. Neither has demonstrated
universal superiority. We retain the original selection instead of choosing again after seeing those errors.
   Our selected temperature path, driven by the recent-growth concentration rule, reaches 1.25, 1.50
and 2.00◦ C above the 1951–1980 average in 2036, 2048 and 2076. Driving the same relationship with
the quadratic concentration path gives slightly smaller errors. Other models and fitting periods move the
dates. These are crossing dates on conditional annual paths, not established deadlines, sustained warming
dates or Paris targets. The observed association does not isolate cause either.
   The tests extend only ten years ahead, and a range based on earlier temperature errors missed the 2016
observation. There is no guaranteed reliability date or established probability attached to a century-scale
projection. Continued measurement and regular comparison with observations matter more than treating
a calculated date as certain. Planning can compare several assumptions, while evaluating interventions
also requires evidence on emissions, other climate drivers and response delays.
