Climate numbers need more than a date
A concentration figure can be accurate yet answer the wrong question. We examined the records
supplied through 2021 for a mathematical modeling exercise, beginning with what each number
measures.
The supplied CO2 values are annual Mauna Loa averages, despite the question's reference to
March. Their ten-year rise ending in 2004 was 18.74 parts per million, slightly below 18.77 ppm
ending in 2003. That small difference does not settle the separate March claim. NOAA's source
supports about 421 ppm for the monthly average in May 2022; this later observation was not used
to fit our models. [1-4]
Three simple extensions of past concentration changes produce roughly 466-497 ppm in 2050
and 584-688 ppm in 2100. None reaches 685 ppm by 2050. This does not refute OECD: its cited
figure combines greenhouse gases as CO2-equivalent, rather than measuring CO2 alone. [6]
The model changes the answer. A recent-growth rule performed best in our planned development
comparison, but a quadratic curve performed better on the later 2012-2021 assessment. Neither
has demonstrated universal superiority. We retain the original selection rather than choose again
after seeing those errors.
Our selected temperature path, driven by recent-growth CO2, reaches 1.25, 1.50 and 2.00
degrees C above the 1951-1980 average in 2036, 2048 and 2076. Driving the same
relationship with the quadratic concentration path gives slightly smaller errors. Other models and
fitting periods move the dates. These are conditional paths, not established deadlines, sustained
warming dates or Paris targets. The observed association also does not isolate cause.
Tests extend only ten years ahead, and the temperature error reference missed 2016. They
establish no guaranteed reliability date or probability for a century-scale projection.
The useful response is continued measurement, consistent definitions and regular comparison of
forecasts with observations. Planning should examine several assumptions. Evaluating
interventions additionally requires evidence on emissions, other climate drivers and response
delays; these two records cannot quantify policy benefits.
