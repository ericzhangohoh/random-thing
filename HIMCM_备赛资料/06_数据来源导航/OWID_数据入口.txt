One in ten people in the world is over 65 — how does this compare in the youngest and oldest countries?
Hannah Ritchie
One in four people in the world is younger than 15. Just one in ten is 65 or older.
But there are huge differences in this distribution across countries. In the chart, I’ve shown these global figures alongside the country with the largest share of children, the Central African Republic, and the one with the largest share of older people, Japan.
In the Central African Republic, half of the population is under 15. Being over 65 is pretty rare: just 2% of the population.
Japan is the opposite. Being a child or adolescent puts you in the minority. Almost one-third of the population is 65 and older, and that share is
growing quickly
.
These differences in age distribution matter a lot for a country’s infrastructure, socioeconomic conditions, and public spending — from pensions and healthcare to childcare and education.

October 29
How is life at different levels of GDP per capita?
Max Roser
The chart shows the relationship between GDP (gross domestic product) per capita —
a measure of average incomes
in a country — and people’s living conditions.
Where GDP per capita is higher, living conditions are far better in many ways. Where average incomes are higher, children die less often, fewer of them are born dead, mothers die less often, fewer children suffer from hunger, doctors can focus on fewer patients, people live longer, more people can read, children have better access to education and better learning outcomes, more people have access to electricity and clean drinking water, and people are happier with their lives.
The disparity between countries with a low and a high GDP per capita is enormous. Looking at the first panel, for example, you can see that a child born in one of the poorest countries is more than 20 times as likely to die as a child born in one of the richest.
In my article “What is growth?”, I have written about why a high GDP per capita is so important for our living standards and how economic growth is possible
October 27
In China, more than one in five workers is still employed in agriculture
Esteban Ortiz-Ospina
China
makes and exports
more goods than any other country. So, perhaps unsurprisingly, when I think about China’s economy, the first thing that comes to mind is manufacturing — increasingly at the technological frontier in electric vehicles, batteries, and solar panels.
China’s position in manufacturing follows decades of economic transformation. The chart shows one aspect of this: the decline in agricultural employment.
At the turn of the 21st century, around half of all Chinese workers were employed in agriculture. One generation later, that share had more than halved, reaching 22% in 2025.
I find it striking how far the share of workers in agriculture has fallen, and just as striking how high it remains. Agriculture employs more than a fifth of China’s workers, while accounting for
less than 7%
of its GDP.

October 24
The world is getting more of its electricity from renewables but less from nuclear power
Hannah Ritchie and Pablo Arriagada
If the world is to reduce its carbon emissions and tackle climate change, it needs to move away from fossil fuels to low-carbon power.
There are two key sources of low-carbon electricity: renewables — which include solar, wind, hydropower, and others — and nuclear power.
While rapid growth
in solar and wind
has increased the amount of electricity coming from renewables, a lack of enthusiasm for nuclear means it’s playing a shrinking role in the global mix.
In the chart, you can see the share of global electricity coming from fossil fuels, renewables, and nuclear since 1985. Since 2000, nuclear and renewables have followed very different trajectories. Back then, both accounted for a similar share of global electricity. But in 2025, renewables made up more than three times as much: 34% compared to 9%.
The total amount of electricity produced by nuclear plants is
about the same
as it was two decades ago. But because the world produces much more electricity overall, its share of the electricity mix has declined.

October 22
No matter what poverty line you choose, the share of the global population below it has declined
Max Roser
The World Bank’s International Poverty Line is an extremely low poverty line. Set at $3 per day, it draws attention to the living conditions of the poorest people on the planet.
In the chart above, you can see how the share below this poverty line has declined over the last generation. In the year 2000, 36% of the world’s population lived on less than $3 per day. In 2024, it was 10%.
But is progress against global poverty only happening when measured against such a very low poverty line?
The chart gives the answer. The entire blue line is below the entire dark red line. This tells us that no matter what line you choose, the share below it has declined globally.
Progress against higher poverty lines has been large, too. The share living on less than $5 a day has declined from 57% to 25%, and the share living on less than $10 a day has declined from 74% to 53%.
But on the right-hand side of the chart, we see that most people still live on low incomes: 81% of people in the world live on less than $30 a day, or about $900 a month.
As I wrote in a short article, the history of the end of poverty has only just begun
October 20
At the peak of the HIV epidemic, AIDS caused more than half of all deaths in some countries
Hannah Ritchie and Pablo Arriagada
The HIV epidemic — which started in the early 1980s and progressed rapidly in the 1990s — has had lasting impacts that continue today. More than
800,000 people
still die from HIV/AIDS every year.
But some countries were hit much harder than others.
In the chart, you can see the share of all deaths in a given year caused by HIV/AIDS in four countries that were particularly badly affected.
At the height of the epidemic, more than half of all deaths in Zimbabwe and Botswana, and over 40% in South Africa and Namibia, were caused by the disease.
Thankfully, interventions to prevent the spread of HIV and treatments such as
antiretroviral therapy
have reduced death rates across the world. But about one in five deaths in these countries was still caused by AIDS in 2023.

October 17
How many people in the world identify with a particular religion?
Hannah Ritchie
Religious compositions vary a lot from country to country.
In my own country
, the United Kingdom, Christianity is the most popular, and following no religion is becoming increasingly common, particularly among young people. But I struggle to get a sense of how these differences stack up globally.
The Pew Research Center
publishes estimates
of religious affiliation across the world. You can see the global breakdown in the chart for 2020 (the most recent year available). It shows the number of people who identify with each religion. Pew counts people by the religion they report, regardless of their beliefs or practices.
You can see that Christians are the largest group, with 2.3 billion people, followed by Muslims, with 2 billion. People with no religious affiliation come close behind.
Those not affiliated with any religion are among the largest groups in the world. But this undersells religion’s reach: when we add up all religious groups,
most people
in the world — around three in four — identify with a religion. Those with no religious affiliation are still a minority.

What share of children die before their fifth birthday?
What could be more tragic than the death of a young child? Child mortality, the death of children under the age of five, is still
extremely common
in our world today.
The historical data makes clear that it doesn’t have to be this way: it
is possible
for societies to protect their children and reduce child mortality to very low rates. For child mortality to reach low levels, many things have to go right at the same time:
good healthcare
,
good nutrition
,
clean water and sanitation
,
maternal health
, and high
living standards
. We can, therefore, think of
child mortality
as a proxy indicator of a country’s living conditions.
The chart shows our long-run data on child mortality, which allows you to see how child mortality has changed in countries around the world.

What share of the population lives in extreme poverty?
The UN sets the “International Poverty Line” as a worldwide comparable definition for extreme poverty. Extreme poverty is currently defined as living on less than $3 per day. This indicator, published by the World Bank, has successfully drawn attention to the terrible depths of poverty of the poorest people in the world.
Two centuries ago,
the majority
of the world’s population was extremely poor. Back then, it was widely believed that widespread poverty was inevitable. This turned out to be wrong. Economic growth is possible and
makes it possible
for entire societies to leave the deep poverty of the past behind. Whether or not countries are leaving the worst poverty behind can be monitored by relying on this indicator.

How has people’s life expectancy changed over time?
Across the world, people are living longer. In 1900, the global average life expectancy of a newborn was 32 years. By 2021, this had more than doubled to 71 years.
Big improvements were achieved by countries
around the world
. The chart shows that life expectancy has more than doubled in every region of the world. This improvement is not only due to declining child mortality; life expectancy increased
at all ages
.
This visualization shows long-run estimates of life expectancy brought together by our team from several different data sources. It also shows that the
COVID-19 pandemic
led to reduced life expectancy worldwide.

How have CO₂ emissions per capita changed?
The main source of carbon dioxide (CO
2
) emissions is the burning of fossil fuels. It is the primary
greenhouse gas
causing
climate change
.
Globally, CO
2
emissions have remained at just below 5 tonnes per person for over a decade. Between countries, however, there are large differences, and while emissions are rapidly increasing in some countries, they are rapidly falling in others.
The source for this CO
2
data is the Global Carbon Budget, a dataset we update yearly as soon as it is published. In addition to these production-based emissions, they publish consumption-based emissions for the last three decades, which can be viewed in our
Greenhouse Gas Emissions Data Explorer
.

How do average incomes compare between countries around the world?
GDP per capita is a very comprehensive measure of people’s
average income
. This indicator reveals how large the inequality between people in different countries is. In the poorest countries, people live on less than $1,000 per year, while in rich countries, the average income is more than 50 times higher.
The data shown is sourced from the Maddison Project Database. Drawing together the careful work of hundreds of economic historians, the particular value of this data lies in the historical coverage it provides. This data makes clear that the vast majority of people in all countries
were poor
in the past. It allows us to understand when and how the
economic growth
that made it possible
to leave
the deep poverty of the past behind was achieved.

What share of the population is suffering from hunger?
Hunger
has been a severe problem for most of humanity throughout history. Growing enough food to feed one’s family was a constant struggle in daily life. Food shortages, malnutrition, and
famines
were common around the world.
The UN’s Food and Agriculture Organization publishes global data on undernourishment, defined as not consuming enough calories to maintain a normal, active, healthy life. These minimum requirements vary by a person’s sex, weight, height, and activity levels. This is considered in these national and global estimates.
The world has made much progress in reducing global hunger in recent decades. But we are still far away from an end to hunger, as this indicator shows. Tragically, nearly one in ten people still do not get enough food to eat and in recent years — especially during the pandemic — hunger levels have increased.

When has literacy become a widespread skill?
Literacy is a foundational skill. Children need to learn to read so that they can read to learn. When we fail to teach this foundational skill, people have fewer opportunities to lead the rich and interesting lives that a good education offers.
The historical data shows that only a very small share of the population, a tiny elite, was able to read and write. Over the course of the last few generations, literacy levels increased, but
it remains
an important challenge for our time to provide this foundational skill to all.
At Our World in Data,
we investigated
the strengths and shortcomings of the available data on literacy. Based on this work, our team brought together the long-run data shown in the chart by combining several different sources, including historical and recent UNESCO data and a range of research publications.

Where do people lack access to even the most basic electricity supply?
Light at night
makes it possible to get together after sunset;
mobile phones
allow us to stay in touch with those far away; the refrigeration of food reduces food waste; and
household appliances
free up time from household chores. Access to electricity improves people’s living conditions in many ways.
The World Bank data on the world map captures whether people have access to the
most basic
electricity supply — just enough to provide basic lighting and charge a phone or power a radio for 4 hours per day.
It shows that, especially in several African countries, a large share of the population lacks the benefits that basic electricity offers. No radio and no light at night.
