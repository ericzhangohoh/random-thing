## Architecture: compare policies on several explicit outcomes

The paper evaluates schedules by completed trips, occupied campsite-nights, average crossovers per trip and deviation from a target duration distribution. It studies a simple insertion baseline, a daily priority method, a continuous density-adjustment method and a sequence-growth method that prohibits crossovers. The main value is the comparison: a schedule with high utilization can perform poorly on solitude or on serving the intended mix of trip lengths.

Two schedule representations are defined: trip-by-night locations and campsite-by-night occupants (pp.3–4). Each supports a different audit—individual itinerary feasibility versus resource collisions. Maintaining both views, generated from one authoritative schedule, is a useful workflow pattern.

The final recommendation is conditional. The priority model serves more trips while permitting overtaking; the sequence-growth model sacrifices utilization to avoid overtaking. This is more informative than declaring one algorithm universally best. However, the text sometimes calls any collision-free schedule “optimal,” conflating feasibility with optimization. The objective order and the acceptable tradeoff need to remain explicit throughout the paper.

## Model choices and their justifications

**Sequential insertion baseline.** Draw a trip duration, try launch dates and approximately equal daily progress, and reject a duration if no insertion is found. The authors observe that booking a whole long itinerary early can block later requests and that the method ignores crossings (pp.6–7). These observations motivate a more coordinated daily update. The baseline is excluded from formal testing, so the claimed improvement over it is not quantified.

**Daily priority method.** Trips with larger remaining-distance/remaining-days ratios usually receive camps first. On some days the list is shuffled instead of sorted; the paper reports useful sorting probabilities between 0.3 and 0.6. Alternative camps are ranked by whether they introduce crossovers and by closeness to ideal progress (pp.7–9, 17–18). The rationale is that urgent trips have less slack, while randomization can reduce systematic disadvantage to longer trips.

The removal of infeasible trips changes the output distribution. The authors explicitly recognize two mechanisms: longer trips tend to have lower urgency scores and have more nights on which a placement can fail. They compensate by proposing a request mix skewed toward longer durations. This is a valuable example of distinguishing the requested distribution from the accepted distribution rather than assuming they remain equal.

**Continuous density adjustment.** Initially move each boat to its desired mileage, then adjust nearby boats until a one-to-one campsite assignment is possible (pp.9–11). The intended advantage is to treat simultaneous requests symmetrically rather than privilege one ordering. The central approximation is a continuous relaxation followed by discrete assignment. It needs separate checks that the continuous iteration converges and that the mapping back to camps preserves travel limits, deadlines and uniqueness.

**Sequence-growth method.** Start from a structured sequence of trip lengths, insert additional trips while preserving launch/finish ordering, pack trajectories toward a central configuration, and then spread them in time (pp.11–13). The reasoning is that overtaking is constrained by the relative order of launches and finishes. Two initial sequences are compared, showing substantial initialization dependence. Saturation means no further trip can be inserted by the tested procedure; it does not prove a global optimum or even optimality under replacement and reordering moves.

## The physical assumptions materially affect the comparison

The main experiments largely defer vessel assignment: a trip requiring more than an oar boat can travel is assigned a motor boat afterward (pp.4–5). This can produce a schedule conditional on flexible vessel choice, but it does not guarantee a requested propulsion mix or a limited motor fleet. The displayed vessel-count table contains no duration data and therefore does not establish the claimed strong association between vessel type and trip length.

The eight-hour, 64-mile motor limit is treated as guaranteed travel despite being based on average speed. Weather, water level, launch capacity, labor and daytime crowding are omitted. These are explicit assumptions, not evidence that they are irrelevant. The paper's partial mixed-propulsion extension admits that the final spreading step was not adapted to the lower flexibility of oar boats (p.13). Its illustrated extension should not be treated as a fully validated operational solution.

The observed-gap histogram is strongly skewed with many small gaps and a long tail, while the simulation uses uniform spacing (p.5). A uniform grid is a simplifying scenario; the histogram does not justify treating every observed gap as equal. Endpoint conventions also need reconciliation: the tested counts are obtained by dividing river length by spacing, whereas the model defines camps between launch and exit. The density example uses camps only between miles ten and 215, making it a different geometry from a full-length equal grid.

## Density equations need correction before reuse

The printed pair contribution is `sign((dj−di)/D) − (dj−di)/D`, summed for boat `j`; the text then subtracts this sum from `dj` (p.10). For an isolated pair with `dj>di` and separation smaller than `D`, the contribution for the downstream boat is positive, so subtraction moves it toward the upstream boat. The upstream boat moves toward the downstream boat as well. This is attraction rather than the intended separation. The sign or update convention must be reconciled; no code is available to determine what produced the reported example.

The contribution is dimensionless while positions are described in miles, so a displacement scale or a consistent nondimensional coordinate system is also needed. A two-boat fixture would expose both issues. The algorithm further needs a step-size rule, convergence criterion, boundary projection, maximum-travel constraints and a defined assignment procedure. Near-unit separation alone does not guarantee that naive rounding gives every boat a distinct valid campsite.

The example figure reports 1,978 trips with 200 camps, while the comparison table reports 1,811 for the density method. These may be distinct runs, but the configuration or seed difference is not supplied. They should not be merged into one reproducible benchmark.

## Comparative evidence and what it supports

The main table compares three campsite counts—45, 150 and 225—and two target duration distributions (p.14). It includes two sequence initializations, a priority result and one separate density result. At 225 camps under a uniform target, the source reports:

- Sequence initialization 1: 1,707 trips, 51.82 percent open camps and zero crossovers.
- Sequence initialization 2: 2,107 trips, 46.80 percent open camps and zero crossovers, but substantially larger distribution deviation.
- Priority method: 2,911 trips, 16.55 percent open camps and 28.93 crossovers per trip.

This table makes the tradeoff inspectable. The authors correctly explain that the second sequence's greater throughput comes partly from serving shorter trips, not simply from a better arrangement of the same demand. The duration histograms and campsite-use profiles reinforce that explanation (pp.15–16).

The target-distribution error is labeled a variance but is not defined precisely enough to reproduce, including the displayed scale factor. The formula for “open campsite percentage” is also ambiguous in the definitions. Both metrics need explicit numerators, denominators and weighting. Comparing trip count with occupied-night count additionally requires the accepted mean duration, because they need not move together.

The claim that crossovers increase exponentially is not established by three campsite-count cases. The table supports an increase, but distinguishing exponential from linear or other scaling requires more points and an appropriate fit. Likewise, zero crossovers in the reported sequence schedules is a feasibility observation under the modeled trajectories, not a proof that every implementation of the spreading step preserves the condition.

No replication counts, seeds, uncertainty intervals or small-instance optimality comparisons are supplied. The source reports checking that no campsite hosts two trips on a night, but does not provide equally explicit checks for every leg's speed, exact trip duration and the partially implemented vessel assignment. The three policies also use different tuned request distributions and initial structures, which should be recorded before treating their differences as algorithm-only effects.

## Important limitations preserved in the discussion

The authors note that removing high-crossover trips disproportionately removes long trips and loses roughly 10–15 percent of campsite coverage (p.17). This is useful evidence that an apparently simple repair can alter who is served. They also acknowledge that the priority method leaves holes when earlier reservations are removed and is sensitive to arrival load, sorting probability and input mix.

The sequence method's limitations include strong initialization dependence, some travel legs near the speed limit, and uneven launch/arrival counts. These qualifications should survive into the memo, which otherwise claims the algorithms can generate any desired ratio with fairly uniform launches. Combining the models across time windows is plausible, but trips spanning a boundary require joint conflict and crossing checks; concatenating schedules is not automatically feasible.

## Transferable workflow rules

1. Define feasibility separately from optimality and list all outcome metrics before comparing methods.
2. Keep both trip-level and resource-level views of a schedule.
3. Measure how admission and rejection change the accepted demand distribution.
4. Compare algorithms using matched physical assumptions and saved input configurations.
5. Check the sign, units and convergence of a continuous relaxation before rounding it into a discrete plan.
6. Distinguish insertion saturation from improvement under replacement or global optimality.
7. Report throughput together with duration mix, utilization and encounter burden.
8. Treat post hoc resource assignment as a modeling assumption that must satisfy actual resource demand.
9. Carry source limitations and unfinished extensions into the final stakeholder recommendation.
10. Verify compatibility when combining policies across time periods.
