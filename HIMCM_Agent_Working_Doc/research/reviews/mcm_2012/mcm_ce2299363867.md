## Architecture: use different models to answer different management choices

The paper first develops a restrictive equal-speed baseline, then studies three management approaches: decentralized boats following local rules, centrally assigned itineraries, and a probabilistic model of extra travel before finding a campsite. Each approach corresponds to a different amount of managerial control and a different operational promise. The memo makes the choice depend on whether customers follow reserved stops and how much extra-travel risk managers accept (pp.1–4).

This is a useful reason to build multiple models. They do not merely provide alternative numerical techniques; they expose a tradeoff between tourist autonomy, itinerary compliance, campsite utilization and the risk of searching too long. The paper also makes time of day explicit. Travel windows, eight-hour search triggers, ten-hour failure thresholds and sunset create constraints that a daily campsite-only model could miss.

The authors report broad agreement around 1,150–1,250 seasonal trips at about 100 camps, with an itinerary variant approaching 1,400. Agreement is an interesting cross-check, but it is not independent empirical validation: the approaches share uniform spacing, demand, trip-mix and travel assumptions, and their failure metrics are not identical. Those differences must be reconciled before treating the similar totals as confirmation.

## Baseline and simulation: preserve units and operational definitions

The baseline rescales hours, speed and campsite spacing by a full day and river length, then counts reachable campsites (pp.8–10). It constructs synchronized waves of identical boats and derives launch times so they stop together. This supplies a simple benchmark whose geometry can be checked by hand.

The baseline's daily launch bound does not by itself establish that every boat also satisfies the assumed six-day trip duration or the finite season boundary. Its derivatives describe the continuous relaxation of a floored count, not the derivative of the integer-valued schedule everywhere. These are manageable limitations if the baseline is labeled accordingly.

The one-minute agent simulation samples departure time, propulsion and duration; each boat sets a daily distance goal based on remaining river length and remaining nights. A boat begins looking for an open camp after reaching its goal, traveling eight hours or approaching sunset. A failure means traveling after sunset or for more than ten hours (pp.11–12). This rule-based description is concrete enough to identify useful verification cases: no competitors, two boats reaching one camp together, the last available camp, and a boat with no feasible remaining daylight.

Earlier departures and longer minimum trips for slow boats are evaluated as operational interventions. The source also examines load, campsite count and utilization at individual sites. The campsite-level diagnostic is valuable because an acceptable river-wide average can hide a concentrated bottleneck.

The reported evidence has unresolved inconsistencies. Figure 2 is captioned as a failure percentage but displays campsite utilization and repeats the utilization plot in Figure 3 (pp.13–14). The failure-versus-arrivals plot shows a noticeable failure rate at six to seven daily arrivals, while another plot and the narrative support a less-than-one-percent capacity near that range (pp.15–17). These may reflect different optimized configurations, but the configurations are not recorded distinctly enough to establish that. The per-campsite utilization discussion also switches between campsite index and miles; its claim of low utilization before the twentieth mile and after the ninetieth mile does not follow directly from the plotted campsite-index profile.

Only the utilization profile is explicitly described as covering five simulated seasons. Repetition counts, random seeds and uncertainty intervals for the principal risk threshold are not provided. A low observed failure fraction needs a sample-size-based uncertainty assessment before it supports a one-percent operational promise.

## Itinerary construction: prioritize inflexible requests, then distinguish rejection from failure

The itinerary heuristic sorts slow boats first because their reachable set is smaller. It chooses the available campsite closest to each boat's daily target. If a boat cannot be placed or cannot finish in time, it is removed from the itinerary entirely (pp.17–22). Prioritizing the least flexible requests is a clear, problem-specific justification for a greedy rule.

Removing an infeasible request before publishing the final itinerary can produce a feasible accepted schedule. It does not mean every attempted request is served. The summary's guarantee therefore applies only to accepted boats, assuming they follow the final itinerary and camps remain available. Rejected requests, accepted throughput and on-river failures must be reported separately. The algorithm also needs to specify whether earlier reservations of a removed boat are released and whether other requests are reconsidered afterward.

The paper deliberately increases attempted load above the low-rejection operating point and reports roughly 1,360–1,400 accepted boats, with very high utilization. This is a useful distinction between a conservative service level and a dense schedule obtained by selecting among excess requests. It does not establish an absolute upper bound: the output is from one heuristic, the mix of accepted durations matters, and no unrestricted optimization bound is supplied.

The diagnostic plots show average duration and speed among rejected boats. They identify a motorboat disadvantage near the seven-per-day operating point, consistent with giving slow boats priority. At higher loads, an average rejected speed near six miles per hour does not by itself establish fairness or independence from type; compare rejection rates conditional on each requested type and duration. The figure error bars are visible, but their meaning and replication basis are not specified clearly.

The printed target-index range is fixed at `i=1,...,6` while trip duration is variable (p.18), another implementation detail that must be resolved before reuse. The overall season is described as May 1 through October 31 and also as 180 days; those conventions need reconciliation. Trips beginning near closure require an explicit treatment rather than automatic multiplication of daily launches by season length.

## Probability model: a useful question with important distributional gaps

The third model asks for the probability that a boat must travel more than a cutoff distance after deciding to stop (pp.23–27). Exponential desired travel distances motivate a homogeneous Poisson process. Conditioning on a fixed number of stops over the river produces uniform unordered locations; sorting them yields the planned stop sequence. The authors then use a steady mix of launch cohorts to argue for a uniform spatial population on a typical night.

The relevant distinctions are crucial. Conditional on the number of stops and the fixed river length, the gaps are no longer independent exponential variables. A particular trip-age cohort has an order-statistic distribution. An equal-weight mixture of all ranks can have uniform marginal density, but the nightly collection is not automatically independent and identically distributed uniform draws. That stronger claim is required for the simple binomial occupancy calculations. Startup, end-of-season effects and speed-limited feasible legs further change the spatial distribution.

The printed formulas also need direct probability checks:

- The second event conditions on two boats falling in a combined interval, but then uses the unnormalized subinterval probability `w`; the conditional probability should reflect the ratio of subinterval length to combined length.
- The first event is described as two boats in the first span and none in the adjacent span, while its displayed complement excludes only the first span. The event description and formula are not identical.
- Exact configurations containing two competitors do not on their own give the tail probability of being blocked beyond a distance. Cases with more competitors and mutually exclusive event accounting must be included. The general expression is not supplied.
- The text labels a binomial parameter as two, which cannot be a probability (p.25). The surrounding formula indicates a notation error, but the executable interpretation is unavailable.
- The mixed-speed extension adjusts the threatening spatial region for overtaking. This is a useful geometric idea, but it assumes a compatible timing convention for when boats begin searching. Desired stop location alone does not determine arrival order when boats stop at different times.

An exact enumeration of a tiny river and an independent event simulation would be better verification than relying solely on smooth probability curves. Tests should include zero competitors, all-slow boats, all-fast boats, very sparse camps and near-exit positions.

## Appendix plots change the interpretation of the recommendation

Every probability plot is evaluated at the same location, 28.125 miles from launch (one-eighth of the river), rather than averaged across location or evaluated at a worst-case location (pp.30–35). The source's claim about the average customer therefore needs an additional location-distribution calculation.

The 99-campsite plots label spacing as 1.25 miles while also calling it one-hundredth of a 225-mile river. These numbers are inconsistent; one-hundredth is 2.25 miles. The active numerical spacing cannot be established without code. This discrepancy directly affects any capacity read from those plots.

The claim that both boat types generally prefer competition from their own type is not supported uniformly by the curves. In the medium- and close-spacing fast-boat plots, increasing the fraction of fast competitors increases the displayed probability of additional travel. Several curves cross in the widely spaced case. Recommendations must specify the parameter range and agree with the actual plotted ordering rather than compressing the result into a universal slogan.

The final probabilistic recommendation permits 77 boats on the river and converts this to 1,155 seasonal trips using an average twelve-day trip. This is a steady-state inventory-to-throughput approximation. It does not incorporate startup or drain-down and does not prove a season-wide risk guarantee. Moreover, fourteen additional miles means a different additional travel time for four-mile-per-hour and eight-mile-per-hour boats; a common distance threshold is not automatically a common safety threshold.

## How to use this paper in the working document

The constructive lesson is to pair each model with the operational choice it illuminates: local behavior, centrally accepted reservations or a risk-limited service policy. A coherent comparison should hold common inputs fixed, record the exceptions, and compare the same endpoint. The paper's daylight and crowding considerations are useful additions to a purely combinatorial schedule, while its cross-model agreement shows why a reviewer must also inspect shared assumptions.

1. Establish a simple benchmark and state which constraints it relaxes.
2. Use explicit behavioral rules when autonomy is part of the problem.
3. Prioritize requests with fewer feasible choices, but test the heuristic against alternatives.
4. Separate rejected requests from accepted trips and failures during execution.
5. Distinguish uniform marginals from independent uniform observations, and unconditional processes from conditioned ones.
6. Define the risk event, its denominator, time horizon and spatial aggregation before reporting a threshold.
7. Label every experiment with its configuration, repetitions and uncertainty calculation.
8. Compare models under matched assumptions and metrics before treating agreement as evidence.
9. Audit graph captions, units, curve ordering and the final memo against saved outputs.
