## Architecture and the reason for its selection

The paper begins with assumptions, customer-satisfaction criteria and desired model properties before selecting a daily cellular-automaton-like simulation. Its central argument is that flexible trip requests require schedules that adapt to current campsite occupancy. Each cell represents a campsite, and each visitor object represents a rafting party with a vessel type, requested duration, current position and remaining time. Daily updates load demand, move parties, resolve conflicts and calculate outcomes (pp.3–13).

The representation makes the scarce resource explicit: at most one party occupies a campsite each night. It also makes local movement rules easy to exchange while keeping the rest of the simulator fixed. This modular experimental structure is the paper's most transferable strength. Calling the representation a cellular automaton adds little by itself; the actual justification is the alignment between campsite states, daily transitions and the desired conflict checks.

The desired movement is remaining distance divided by remaining days, rounded into campsite steps and clipped to travel limits. A party's continuous urgency ratio determines which conflicting request retains its preferred camp. Others search for the nearest reachable free camp, initially in a random direction, then in the other direction if necessary. The preferred direction reverses on later bumps to reduce persistent displacement (pp.10–12).

Recomputing urgency is intended to correct earlier deviations; two-way search is intended to use otherwise stranded capacity. These are concrete mechanism-based justifications. The paper tests them separately, which is stronger evidence than merely asserting that an adaptive algorithm is appropriate.

## What the experiments establish

Seven implementations vary daily recalculation, whether conflicts are resolved, and whether displacement is bidirectional, always upstream, always downstream or randomly unidirectional (p.12). The first comparison at 100 camps and mean daily arrival rate five reports 756 completions with recalculation and bumping, versus 753 without recalculation, while off-schedule completions rise from 2.1 to 42.3 percent. Removing bumping sharply reduces completions to 182 or 190 (p.14). The natural interpretation is that recalculation chiefly repairs duration accuracy in this configuration, while conflict repair chiefly increases acceptance.

The second comparison reports that always-upstream displacement produces fewer interactions and fewer off-schedule completions, but many more rejections. Thus the preferred implementation represents a tradeoff, not dominance on every outcome. An explicit objective hierarchy or Pareto comparison would support the recommendation more precisely than the paper's description of the method as ideal and optimal.

The plotted surfaces vary campsite count and arrival intensity (pp.15–18). They show decreasing rejection and timing problems as the number of camps increases, plus a capacity plateau across much of the displayed domain. The plotted ridges and discrete irregularities also warn against replacing these results with a smooth universal claim. There are no reported replication counts, uncertainty intervals or small-instance exact benchmarks. A table's repeated configuration yields slightly different outputs in the two experiments, plausibly because of stochastic runs, but saved seeds and sampling variation are unavailable.

The paper reports its maximum tested capacity as about 1,102 completed parties per season at 180 camps and mean daily input 15, with 1.79 percent rejected and 0.67 percent off schedule. This is a reported simulation result, not a reproduced optimum. It occurs at the upper tested arrival rate, so the sweep does not establish a global maximum over demand intensity. If campsite count is itself a decision, construction and maintenance costs or a fixed resource limit are needed before maximizing across it. The discussion suggests lower-cost alternatives, but it does not estimate a cost function; the final discussion also shifts from a 100-camp, 1,025-trip example to a 110-camp, 1,074-trip example.

## Admission, conservation and units require repair

The simulation removes a visitor if no reachable free camp can be found. The formal rejection definition includes removal both before and after loading (p.13). The memo interprets this as advance refusal of a requested trip because the model can be run before departure (p.22). Those are different operational meanings. Knowing requests 24 hours ahead, as assumed earlier, does not establish full-horizon feasibility under later arrivals. A deployed online policy needs an admission test, reserved future capacity or a feasible fallback for an already launched party; deleting that party from the simulation is not such a policy.

This ambiguity also affects the reported capacity and rejection percentages. At a stated mean input of 15 parties per day over 180 days, expected requests are roughly 2,700, while reported completions are roughly 1,102 and rejection is about two percent. Random variation and terminal occupancy are possible, but the paper supplies no complete ledger to reconcile these quantities. The necessary identity is requests = completed + rejected + waiting + still active, with initial inventory and any other removal states defined. This observation identifies missing accounting; it does not prove that a particular unknown realization was impossible.

The words visitor, person, group and boat are used across different sections. The model object is a party occupying a campsite, so its throughput is groups, not individual people unless a party-size model is added. Likewise, 180 times the number of camps is a campsite-night bound, not directly a bound on the number of multiday visitors. Duration and endpoint conventions are needed to translate that resource bound into trip capacity.

The encounter criterion is drawn from a per-person-per-day literature threshold, while the reported metric is an average per visitor over the trip or season. The stricter aggregate comparison may still be favorable, but units and denominators should be aligned explicitly. The text says total interactions stay below ten, yet the selected operating point reports 10.35; this does not necessarily violate the original daily threshold, but it contradicts the stated aggregate claim.

## Feasibility and physical assumptions

The source permits oar speeds of 3–5 mph and motor speeds of 3–10 mph with four to eight travel hours per day, giving broad feasible-distance intervals. Those assumptions differ from treating the problem's nominal speeds as fixed. The supporting itinerary sources justify plausible scenarios rather than validate every simulated leg.

Camps are equally spaced and assumed sufficiently secluded that camp-to-camp and raft-to-camp interactions are zero (pp.6–7, 13). Those zeros arise from assumptions, not measured results. Overtaking is inferred from a reversal of two parties' campsite order between the start and end of a day. Under synchronized constant-velocity movement this is a useful proxy, but launch timing, exits, stops and nonconstant speeds require event-level treatment if physical encounters are the target.

Several implementation details remain underspecified: whether all conflicts of two parties are processed despite the general rule referring to more than two; how ties are broken; whether bump chains terminate; how movement constraints are checked after all displacements; and how first and final legs are represented. Resetting remaining days to one prevents division by zero for late parties, but it does not itself guarantee a feasible finish. These require invariants and small adversarial fixtures before reusing the scheduler.

## Stress tests: valuable structure, limited calibration

The environmental extension reduces usable camps according to `Y − α log(λ)` and shows how increasing the degradation coefficient worsens capacity, lateness and rejection at 110 original camps (pp.19–20). Deliberately breaking an idealizing assumption is a strong workflow move. However, the equation depends on arrival intensity rather than accumulated local use or recovery; it has no daily state despite being described as degradation over time. Its parameters are uncalibrated. The table omits the actual values labeling its four arrival-rate columns, preventing complete configuration reconstruction from the displayed result.

The storm extension suspends movement and loading for a random number of days and advances elapsed trip time. It exposes a mechanism by which external disruptions produce lateness. The source describes tests at four storm probabilities and four arrival rates but does not include their numerical table or plots. Statements that five-percent storms are acceptable are therefore not fully inspectable, and the event clock, overlap treatment and zero-day Poisson events need definition. Customer willingness to accept weather delays is an additional behavioral assumption.

## Transferable workflow rules

1. Select a state representation because it exposes the decision constraints and measurable outcomes.
2. Separate algorithm components and test them with controlled ablations.
3. Preserve tradeoffs when one variant improves acceptance but worsens other metrics.
4. Distinguish advance admission refusal from failure after a resource user has entered the system.
5. Reconcile arrivals, completions, rejection, queues and terminal inventory before reporting capacity.
6. Keep people, groups, boats, trips and occupied resource-days as separate units.
7. Compare a heuristic with a competent baseline; disabling conflict repair is an ablation, not proof against all static optimization.
8. Give every simulation surface its parameter grid, seeds, replications and uncertainty.
9. Stress-test consequential assumptions, but label uncalibrated stress mechanisms as scenarios.
10. Carry assumptions and accounting limits into the management memo rather than converting simulated outcomes into guarantees.
