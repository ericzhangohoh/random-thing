## Strongest reusable architecture and source completion

The strongest contribution is **a model of the operating environment separated from a controller that only receives information available at the current time**. Flight plans, delays, passenger needs and notice times create an event stream; a dispatch policy repeatedly updates escort and chair assignments (pp.18–19). The controller has a planned layer for known requests and a reserve layer for disruptions and short-notice requests (pp.15–17).

This source is candidly incomplete: the abstract and conclusion state that simulation results were not obtained before the deadline, and the authors cannot make specific budget recommendations (pp.1, 19–20). Its empirical component fits and detailed algorithm description were completed; the integrated policy evaluation, optimal resource counts, shift schedule and budget were not. Full review of the paper does not upgrade these proposed results into demonstrated results.

## Approach, justification and cross-module interfaces

The authors simplify the airport to the concourses and gates relevant to connecting passengers. Travel-time matrices capture geometry; congestion and whether an escort is alone, pushing an empty chair or carrying a passenger multiply base travel time (pp.5–7, 12). The load factors are informed by a small original rolling-chair experiment, an inexpensive way to estimate a quantity directly needed by the controller. Three airport sizes are grounded in particular airline gate arrangements (p.6).

Incoming delay is a mixture with a point mass at zero and an exponential positive-delay component, fitted to Bureau of Transportation Statistics data for December 2005 (pp.7–8). This respects the observed distinction between on-time and delayed flights. Layovers use a scaled beta distribution fitted to 286 sampled flight itineraries, with the histogram and fitted curve shown together (pp.10–11). Passenger assistance frequency is estimated through a sequence of demographic conditional proportions (pp.9–10).

Chairs have explicit states: occupied/unoccupied, moving/stationary, and stored/not stored. An escort is released after delivering a passenger, but the wheelchair remains occupied until boarding (pp.12–13). This difference in resource release times justifies separate chair inventory and staff scheduling. Instead of assigning an unsupported monetary value to an obstruction hazard, the authors choose a configurable gate storage capacity, initially two unused chairs (p.14).

Daily-Ordered Escorts greedily chain known passenger transfers into shifts. Zoned Escorts provide local reserve coverage, move chairs toward earlier expected demand, and exchange zone assignments after cross-zone tasks (pp.15–17). Only the next instruction is communicated, while the internal schedule is recalculated as events arrive (pp.17–19). This is a concrete operational reason for rolling replanning rather than one static daily assignment.

**Reviewer interpretation:** the candidate method follows the information structure and state transitions, not merely a generic scheduling label. Planned demand and uncertain demand need different handling; chair rebalancing cannot be reduced to passenger transport. The proposed reserve architecture is reusable even though its cost effectiveness was never tested here.

## Evidence and validation limits

There are two visual empirical fit checks: the positive-delay exponential curve and layover beta curve (pp.8, 11). They are calibration checks against the same samples used to construct the models. No holdout sample, goodness-of-fit statistic, uncertainty interval or tail-specific check is reported. The authors describe their small travel experiment but provide neither observations, sample sizes nor variation (p.12).

The source gives transparent calculations converting annual wages to hourly compensation and aggregate aviation delay costs to a per-minute rate (pp.13–14). These support the scale of assumed inputs but do not prove that average industry delay cost equals the marginal cost of one delayed wheelchair connection.

There are no integrated simulated outcomes, baseline comparisons, controller ablations, feasibility traces, resource-conservation checks, replicate distributions or budget-error calculations. Claims of improved cost efficiency at larger airports and with future assistance demand are qualitative predictions (p.20), not observed model results. No source code is included.

## Static checks and model boundaries

- **Information availability:** p.8 says all delays are unexpected and scheduling may use only scheduled arrivals; p.19 assigns approximately five hours of warning to flight delays. These are different information regimes. The event-to-controller interface must explicitly define which one is used, otherwise the policy can benefit from unintended foreknowledge.
- **Population denominator:** the demographic calculation combines disability prevalence, ability to travel, actual use of air travel and assistance needs (pp.9–10). It treats all nondisabled citizens as available air travelers, then infers a share of airline passengers. A person-level population proportion does not become a flight-passenger proportion without travel frequency, selection and compatible conditioning. The reported one-in-1,850 figure is consequently a rough assumption, not a directly measured airport request rate.
- **Calibration after transformation:** a sampled layover is replaced by the next actual scheduled flight after that duration (p.10). The realized layover distribution is therefore generally longer than the fitted beta draw. It must be checked after this assignment, including end-of-day connections and missed departures.
- **Tail guarantee:** “95% confidence travel time” is used to schedule trips without a travel-time distribution or quantile calculation (p.15). A confidence interval for an estimated mean would not be a 95% prediction bound for an individual trip. Multiple marginal 95% safeguards also do not establish a 95% whole-day reliability target.
- **Greedy optimality and shifts:** the chain starts with the earliest pending arrival and extends to the first feasible next request (pp.15–16). No argument establishes a minimum number of escorts. The instructions stop when total work exceeds four hours; a feasible shift should reject the addition that would exceed the limit or explicitly permit overtime.
- **Reserve sufficiency:** a short travel radius alone does not establish that one zone escort can serve simultaneous requests, cover a zone while away on a cross-zone trip, or deliver every passenger before boarding (pp.16–17). The proposed swap mechanism is operationally useful but requires a busy-state and concurrency test. The paper's necessity/sufficiency language is stronger than its evidence.
- **Marginal versus repeated delay costs:** charging each late passenger a full flight-delay rate can double-count a single delayed plane when several passengers share it. The model also needs to distinguish schedule lateness from extra delay attributable to the assistance service (pp.13–14).
- **Storage feasibility:** the two-chair gate capacity is an anecdotal initial value. A redistribution heuristic must demonstrate compliance during transitions, not merely prioritize clearing excess after it occurs. Existing passenger-occupied chairs and finite safe storage need distinct definitions.

These are static formulation checks; unavailable software was not diagnosed or executed.

## Communication and reusable rules

The paper explains each data input's role and provides an unusually clear diagram of the model/controller feedback loop (p.18). Its conclusion correctly distinguishes intended experiments from completed work. The main lesson for an agent is to preserve that honesty while protecting enough time to deliver a simple tested end-to-end policy.

1. Separate the environment that generates truth from the policy's time-dependent information set.
2. Write resource states and release events before choosing a dispatch algorithm.
3. Use fitted distributions or small measurements for decision-relevant quantities, and validate the distribution again after downstream transformations.
4. Treat unclear monetary harm as an explicit constraint when that is defensible, and test the proposed limit.
5. Pair scheduled service with reserve capacity only after testing concurrent disruptions and the value of the reserve.
6. Require a complete baseline early; a strong proposed controller is not a substitute for resource counts, costs and measured service reliability.
