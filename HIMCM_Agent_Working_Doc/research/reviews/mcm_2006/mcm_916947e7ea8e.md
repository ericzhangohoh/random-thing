## Strongest reusable architecture

The architecture is **service-process map → cost decomposition → depot geometry → demand generation → separate chair/escort resources → hourly resource budget → annual cost recommendation**. Its useful insight is that a wheelchair and an escort are distinct resources: a chair can remain near the passenger while the escort returns, and empty chairs can be returned in batches (pp.15–18). This creates a rebalancing problem in addition to a passenger-service problem.

The paper explicitly links depot position to travel time and travel time to required resources. It also includes maintenance, staff, storage, accident exposure and delay-related lost business rather than optimizing equipment purchase alone (pp.6–10). These are worthwhile interfaces to retain even though several connections are not implemented faithfully in the appendix.

## Approach selection, justification and module links

The authors use annualized costs to put different expenditures on a common scale. They derive a linear ticket-retention response to delay, then replace it with a cubic response whose slope vanishes at both endpoints, arguing that small initial delays and already-extreme delays should have weaker marginal effects (pp.8–10). This is an explicit structural comparison, but the nonlinear curve is a behavioral assumption, not an estimated response.

For simplified rectangular concourses, triangle inequalities show why a middle gate or the rectangle's center minimizes an unweighted sum of Euclidean distances (pp.11–12). The authors recognize that the distance-minimizing location may be a busy pedestrian area and suggest moving storage to a less exposed point at the cost of longer trips. The reusable choice is to expose the travel-versus-obstruction tradeoff; the geometric result applies only to the simplified, equally weighted destinations.

Flight arrivals are described as Poisson, with exponential interarrival times. Passenger assistance needs are tied to airplane capacity, and the text approximates escort requests with a normal distribution (pp.13–14). Loaded and empty travel speeds are distinguished in the distance-to-time interface (pp.15–16). An information system is supposed to provide current requests and available resources; a flowchart iterates chair, escort and return-trip counts until service is achieved (pp.17–18).

The appendix instead supplies fixed arrays of arrival gaps, generates passenger and assistance counts, bins demand hourly, and computes resource counts from workload formulas (pp.23–25). Its final comment accurately calls this “Simplified scheduling.” It does not maintain individual resource locations, jobs, passenger deadlines or a dispatch queue. Therefore the detailed conceptual information-system architecture and the implemented hourly calculation must be recorded as separate levels of completion.

## Reported results and evidence

The authors report daily hourly schedules for three airport sizes (pp.19–21). Peak chair requirements are 13, 36 and 105; peak escort requirements are 11, 30 and 68. Annual total costs are reported as $60,540, $170,880 and $480,900. The resource table makes the recommendation concrete, and the demand plots expose distinct peak periods.

The source cites a Sydney airport arrival estimate and external values for equipment, rent and labor. It does not report a goodness-of-fit test for arrivals, independent validation of assistance rates, repeated simulation distributions, confidence intervals, sensitivity ranges, an out-of-sample day, dispatch-level feasibility traces or comparison with an existing policy. References to real quantities are parameter sources, not validation of the whole system.

The paper claims the cost is lowest because the required resources are indispensable and risk/delay costs are nearly zero (pp.21–22). No lower bound, exhaustive comparison or optimization certificate establishes this. The appendix has neither a delay outcome nor an accident model from which the zero entries could be calculated. The authors themselves acknowledge strong advance-notice assumptions, approximation and a need for better data (pp.22–23).

## Static checks and limits

These checks concern the printed model and appendix. They are not numerical reproduction or assertions about unavailable author software.

- **Cost coupling:** the text identifies that adding equipment/staff raises direct costs while reducing delays (p.10), yet later argues that minimizing each cost separately minimizes their sum (p.22). Separate minimization is not valid for competing objectives unless the minimizers coincide. A joint decision vector and feasible set are required.
- **Maintenance annualization:** annual maintenance is defined as a yearly amount, but equation (1) divides it by equipment lifetime together with the purchase-minus-salvage value (p.6). Annual maintenance should remain outside that lifetime division under the stated definitions.
- **Labor-cost consistency:** salary is $20,000 per escort per year and equation (2) converts required concurrent staff to weekly coverage (pp.4, 7). The small-airport table nevertheless assigns only $14,000 annual labor expense despite requiring multiple escorts for many hours (p.21). No part-time or utilization reconciliation explains the discrepancy.
- **Time-window consistency:** opening hours are 06:30–22:30 on p.4, 12 hours/day in the labor formula on p.7, 06:00–20:00 in the resource table, and bins beginning at 06:30 in the code (p.25). A common operating horizon is necessary before costs and coverage can be compared.
- **Rate versus mean:** p.13 defines the exponential rate through the inverse mean gap, while p.18 gives `lambda = 42 min`. A rate cannot have units of minutes. The appendix's fixed arrival-gap arrays also do not implement exponential draws.
- **Distribution mismatch:** the normal model sets its three-sigma endpoints to zero and the number needing chairs (p.14), which does not follow from the central limit theorem or a binomial variance. The code instead independently assigns escort need with probability one-half (p.25). The bounded discrete distribution and the prose approximation are different models.
- **Geometry scope:** the rectangle proofs use strict triangle inequalities where equality can occur on segments (pp.11–12); non-strict inequalities can repair the relevant lower bound. More importantly, unequal gate demand, walls, restricted storage and pedestrian exposure invalidate a blanket conclusion that the geometric center is optimal.
- **Workload versus schedule:** `n_wheels = ceil(2*l1*Q_av/v1)` is an average workload estimate. It does not prove feasibility when passengers arrive in batches with deadlines. The escort expression adds a travel-workload term to `2*Q_av*v1/(v2*x)`, whose stated dimensions are a rate rather than a concurrent resource count (p.25). Batch transport needs an explicit trip duration and frequency.
- **Rare loss:** a small probability is not a zero expected cost, especially after describing accident or delay consequences as large. Their probability, severity and uncertainty must remain visible instead of setting both terms to zero.

## Communication and reusable rules

The process diagrams and cost table give a reader a useful end-to-end story. The main presentation weakness is that the conceptual flowchart, mathematical distribution assumptions and actual code appear to describe the same simulation while providing materially different behavior.

1. Model consumable capacity, reusable equipment and labor separately when their release times differ.
2. Define every cross-module quantity with its time horizon, unit and state meaning: hourly demand is not a feasible dispatch schedule.
3. Use analytical geometry to establish a tractable baseline, then test the assumptions that make it optimal.
4. Maintain a trace from each cost-table entry to a computed outcome and a consistent annualization rule.
5. Label proposed, simplified and implemented modules explicitly; inspect the appendix against the prose before claiming integrated validation.

Cross-case link: [2006 B868](mcm_4a742ada6f09.md) represents an escort with its wheelchair as a combined dispatched resource and evaluates adaptive insertion policies. This paper highlights the additional state and empty-chair transport needed when those resources are separated. The appropriate architecture depends on the operating policy rather than the airport keyword.
