## Problem structure and strongest reusable architecture

The paper seeks the number of tollbooths that minimizes extra travel time during a rush period. Its distinctive architecture is **derive capacities → classify operating regimes → attach a queue state to each active bottleneck → pass each stage's outflow to the next → compare feasible booth counts across demand scenarios** (pp.6–18).

The most useful contribution is the regime map. Rather than begin with a named queueing algorithm, it identifies which bottleneck can physically become active from the ordering of arrival, plaza, service and exit rates. The same recurrence is then reused with different capacities and time scales. This is an interpretable route from physical structure to model selection, although the detailed formulas and numerical outputs require further verification before operational reuse.

## Architecture and interfaces

1. Decision and metric: booth count `m` is controllable, highway lanes `n` and demand are given. The objective is average additional delay among vehicles entering during congestion, relative to empty-system travel (pp.3–5).
2. Capacities: exit capacity is `kappa = n/theta`; toll capacity is `lambda = m/tau`; pre-booth plaza capacity is `mu = m/theta`. Service time is assumed to satisfy `tau = p*theta` with integer `p > 1` (pp.4, 6).
3. Regime test: `m/n > p` is a necessary condition for a downstream squeeze queue, since booth outflow must be capable of exceeding road capacity. The paper correctly distinguishes this capacity condition from sufficient demand to activate it (pp.6–7).
4. State evolution: each queue accumulates arrivals and subtracts served cars until it clears. Booth congestion caps downstream arrivals; plaza congestion caps booth arrivals. The analysis separates filling, sustained congestion and clearing phases (pp.8–18).
5. Synchronization: booth updates occur at intervals `tau`, other queues at `theta`; switching times are rounded to the next relevant update to connect modules (p.16).
6. Case study: approximate the Dublin Westlink M50 with two road lanes and originally five booths, a four-second travel interval and `p = 2`. Compare booth counts 2–14 under five peak rates from 2,000 to 6,000 cars/hour (pp.21–25).
7. Decision aggregation: assign subjective scenario probabilities centered on 4,000 cars/hour, compute a weighted mean delay, then discuss the operating and capital-cost consequences of competing designs (pp.25–28).

The model's modules have explicit one-way dependencies. An upstream bottleneck shapes the downstream input curve, rather than each queue receiving the original unconstrained arrival rate independently. The assumed sufficiently long plaza removes downstream spillback, which is a substantive boundary on that coupling (p.4).

## Approach and justification

**Author reports:** discrete vehicle spacing and constant speeds justify time-step recurrences; a smooth two-hour demand peak with ±1% random perturbation approximates rush flow (pp.4–6). Capacity ordering selects which queues to model. The authors recognize that a booth count may cease to affect downstream congestion in some regimes because the road itself fixes the output limit (p.10). This explanation is more useful than an unexplained optimal ratio.

**Reviewer interpretation:** the strongest transferable method is the capacity/phase decomposition, followed by explicit handoffs and queue clearing. The case study also shows a useful final decision distinction: two designs with similar delay can have very different implementation costs (p.28).

The chosen behavioral simplifications are large: identical vehicles, equal lane preferences, fixed movement speed and service time, no spillback, and an integer ratio of service and movement intervals. The authors openly identify several as unrealistic (pp.19–20). The added ±1% noise is not a calibrated arrival process; adding randomness alone does not establish realism. The scenario weights are chosen because the authors felt a normal-shaped distribution was plausible, not fitted from observations (p.25).

## Validation and results

**Author-reported results:** the weighted case study favors four or eight booths over the current five. The conclusion prefers closing a booth because adding three requires widening and construction, while acknowledging possible public opposition (pp.25–28). The one-booth-per-road-lane configuration becomes poor as demand rises (pp.26–28).

The numerical tables constitute scenario exploration, not independent validation. No held-out observed delays, explicit conservation results, run counts, uncertainty intervals, timestep convergence or numerical boundary tests are reported. The manuscript claims the two favored booth counts are not significantly different, but supplies no statistical test or distribution of their difference (pp.25, 28). Its conclusion should remain conditional on the constructed model.

The authors' own weakness section questions whether an average-delay objective captures the intended fairness of the system (p.20). That is a valuable reflex: a formally minimized function may still misrepresent the user's goal. Their illustrative numerical comparison has its inequality reversed—one hour divided among 100 users is 0.6 minutes, exceeding 0.5 minutes—but the underlying need to inspect distributional effects remains valid.

## Static audit findings and reuse boundaries

- Page 9 defines total delay with a multiplicative `theta`, then drops that factor in equation (3) while its denominator remains a count of vehicles. The printed average-delay equation needs dimensional reconciliation before reuse.
- Adjacent regimes and time grids need explicit continuity and conservation tests. Later expressions contain notation changes, including a booth sum labeled with `c` rather than `b` (p.13), and a transition written with `D*theta` before the next section uses `D*tau` (p.16). Without the promised appendix, the actual implemented interpretation cannot be checked.
- Numerical tables contain large changes when adding one booth, such as 3.7 to 0.024 to 58.3 minutes at peak 5,000 for 10, 11 and 12 booths (p.24). A surprising nonmonotonic result can be physically meaningful, but requires a queue trace and boundary-case explanation before it supports a design recommendation. Here the phase transitions and formulas need that verification.
- The parameter choice `p = 2` is partly justified by requiring the observed five-booth, two-lane configuration to lie in a downstream-congestion regime (p.21). A workflow should estimate service and movement parameters independently before using the regime prediction as evidence.
- Although the denominator is restricted to arrivals during a congestion episode, that episode can change with the policy. Comparisons need a shared population/time horizon or an explicit explanation of why changing the denominator is appropriate.

These flags limit reliance on the reported optimum. They do not erase the useful decomposition, explicit recurrence construction and candid discussion of objective mismatch.

## Reusable operating rules

1. Derive capacity inequalities before deciding which processes need modeling.
2. Separate a necessary condition from a sufficient operating condition.
3. Pass actual constrained outflow between stages and state when spillback is excluded.
4. Test each regime and every transition, including equality cases and queue clearing.
5. Keep units, time grids and populations consistent across an objective calculation.
6. Treat unexplained jumps in an optimum as diagnostic evidence requiring traces.
7. Label subjective scenario probabilities and test the decision against alternatives.
8. Preserve the distinction between similar modeled performance and statistical equivalence.
9. Compare implementation burden after establishing a supported set of effective options.
