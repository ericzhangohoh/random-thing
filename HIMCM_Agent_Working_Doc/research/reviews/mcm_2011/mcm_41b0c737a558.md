## Strongest reusable architecture

The paper's distinctive contribution is **a general energy functional → a tractable circle family for mechanism discovery → a richer ellipse family with sequential mechanism inclusion → normalized sensitivity →3D reuse through a coordinate transformation → a repeated-trick energy-balance criterion → practical tailoring** (pp.5,8–20). It explains both why the model is simplified and why particular refinements are introduced.

1. A common differential equation describes mechanical energy along an arbitrary cross-sectional curve, using gravity, curvature-dependent normal force and sliding friction. A landing collision determines the initial energy by retaining the tangential component of the incoming motion (pp.8–9).
2. Direct optimization over arbitrary functions is avoided by restricting geometry. A quarter-circle transition gives an analytic expression and makes the effects of width, friction, launch height and landing location interpretable (pp.10–12).
3. A quarter ellipse separates horizontal and vertical scale. The authors compare sliding friction alone, friction plus collision, and friction plus collision plus air resistance (pp.13–15). An unrealistic right-angle optimum in the first case is used as evidence that landing loss is a necessary mechanism, not merely as an inconvenient answer to hide.
4. Dimensionless local sensitivities compare the effects of inputs measured in different units (pp.15–17).
5. The tilted course is reduced to an equivalent planar trajectory so the same energy machinery can be reused. Slope is selected to maintain approximately the same hanging height on successive sides, balancing energy lost in a trick against energy gained downhill (pp.17–19).
6. Safety, construction and preparation time modify the purely mathematical shape (p.20).

## Approach selection and justification

**Explicit author reasoning:** an unrestricted shape functional is difficult to optimize directly, so the circle provides a low-dimensional analytic starting point, followed by a more flexible ellipse (pp.10–12). Collision loss is introduced because omitting it drives the optimum to an implausible corner (p.13). The third dimension matters because downhill motion supplies energy, making repeated tricks possible (p.17). Maintaining similar jump heights is defended as a practical alternative to either losing all airtime or increasing height without bound (p.19).

**Reviewer interpretation:** the paper demonstrates a useful model-development loop: inspect the optimizer's answer, ask which omitted mechanism made the answer unrealistic, add that mechanism, then repeat the same comparison. This is stronger justification than appending unrelated models. The repeated-trick balance also turns “practical” into a partially operational criterion, although stability of that balance under perturbation is not analyzed.

The geometric restriction remains a restriction: the ellipse is not proved optimal among all feasible profiles. The assumed trajectory lies in one plane and does not allow active steering. Final-energy maximization is only an adequate proxy for vertical air if launch geometry and the allocation of velocity are controlled. The paper acknowledges the absence of rider motion and the possibility that another curve family performs better (p.21).

## Assumptions, inputs and outputs

The rider is a point mass moving directly on a rigid track; landing is instantaneous and perfectly inelastic in the normal direction; initial descent is vertical from rest; snow friction is constant (pp.6–8). The rigid-track approximation is supported by a construction description from the International Ski Federation, whereas the point-mass choice is justified by analytical focus and reduced biomechanical complexity (pp.7–8). These are different kinds of justification and should remain distinguishable.

The ellipse example uses half-width9m, course height5.4m, initial height9m, mass75kg, friction coefficient0.1 and landing offset0.5m. The numerical ODE uses forward Euler with spatial step10⁻⁴m; a search described as bisection selects ellipse parameters (p.13). Linear air resistance with area0.5m² is added, and the damping coefficient is varied from0 to1.5Ns/m³ before choosing0.75 (pp.14–15). The source cited for that coefficient is a weather reference, but the paper does not provide enough derivation to independently establish its suitability for snowboard aerodynamic drag.

Reported outputs are a circle radius3.831m under the specified reference case (p.12); ellipse axes approximately4.19m and5.4m in2D (p.15); and axes4.42m and5.4m at the3D balance slope17.1° (p.19). The practical recommendation changes the slope to17.5° and lowers the near-edge angle from nearly90° toward85° (pp.20,22). These are author-reported results, not reproduced measurements.

## Cross-module connections

The reusable handoff is **geometry → derivatives and curvature → collision energy → integrated energy → shape optimum**. The3D layer modifies geometry through a coordinate transformation, then reuses the same evaluator (p.18). It assumes about20m of downhill travel per trick based on course length and6–7 tricks per run (p.17). That distance is an input assumption; it is not jointly solved with rider steering.

The slope module solves a different design condition: final height equals initial height. It fits the simulated height-versus-slope relationship with a cubic spline and finds its intersection with9m (p.19). This should be described as a selected equilibrium condition, not as the same maximization objective used in every earlier section. The final safety and twist adjustments are qualitative and are not rerun through a fully coupled rider model.

## Validation and sensitivity actually present

The paper offers mechanism-ablation plots and extensive parameter-response graphs. Circle optimization predicts width independence analytically and displays that result numerically (pp.11–12). Ellipse ablation shows how collision changes the optimum from a boundary to an interior horizontal scale (pp.13–14). Drag affects energy more than selected shape in the displayed range (p.15).

Local sensitivity coefficients include0.011 for damping and0.993 for snow friction at their stated reference values; height/energy sensitivity magnitudes are larger (pp.16–17). These help prioritize input knowledge, but do not constitute global uncertainty propagation or prove robustness across combined changes. The paper claims consistency with real course dimensions and a familiar slope (pp.20–22), not an independent trajectory validation. Some dimensions are already supplied from the same standards, so this agreement is partly a plausibility comparison rather than a fully independent test. No numerical convergence table, measured landing dataset or runnable source code is included.

## Bounded independent checks

The printed landing-speed equation on p.9 contains a square root of `2m(h−y0)/g`; with m denoting mass, this lacks velocity dimensions. The following energy expression has the expected gravitational-energy dimensions, suggesting a transcription issue in the speed equation. It must be rederived before code reuse; this review did not determine which version produced the simulations.

The sensitivity prose says greater initial height leads to larger optimal horizontal axis, but its reported coefficient is negative and Figure11 decreases (p.16). Figure13 is captioned as course-height sensitivity yet its plotted horizontal label is initial height, so the stated positive course-height sensitivity cannot be confidently verified from that visual (p.17). These are specific traceability problems, not evidence that every sensitivity result is wrong. They motivate checking labels, derivative signs and interpretation together.

## Transferable workflow rules

- Use a low-dimensional family to expose mechanisms before expanding the design space.
- Treat an implausible optimum as a diagnostic: inspect omitted losses and missing constraints.
- Keep the evaluator constant while adding one mechanism at a time, so changes in the optimum have an interpretable cause.
- Normalize sensitivities when comparing parameters with different units, and state the reference point.
- Distinguish maximizing a single event from sustaining repeated events; the latter may require an equilibrium or stability condition.
- Separate inputs taken from existing design standards from genuinely independent validation targets.
- Recompute the design after practical tailoring, or explicitly mark those adjustments as judgment pending a coupled check.

The sequence of comparative curves is effective communication: the reader can see which added mechanism changes the answer and which merely changes the performance level. That explanatory use of figures is worth transferring, while the caption/sign inconsistencies show why visual QA belongs in the workflow.
