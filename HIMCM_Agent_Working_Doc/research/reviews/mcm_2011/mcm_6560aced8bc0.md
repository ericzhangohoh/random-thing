## Problem architecture and positive modeling lessons

The paper organizes the half-pipe problem around three explicit objectives: maximum vertical air, maximum rotation, and a practical compromise informed by judging scores (pp. 1–3). It first defines a geometric family—flat bottom with circular transitions—then allocates a role to each design parameter: platform height supplies entry speed, transition angle sets takeoff direction, flat width allows speed recovery, and course length accommodates repeated tricks (pp. 4–6, 10–16). This is more actionable than treating the entire shape as an unconstrained curve. A working document can borrow the decomposition while checking each physical link independently.

**Energy baseline and repeated maneuvers.** The authors express vertical gain in terms of athlete work and frictional loss, distinguish flat and curved contact, and draw the path between successive flight phases (pp. 7–10). The idea that a useful course must permit repeated recovery, not only one ideal jump, is important. They introduce a finite recovery distance: once the rider has regained the assumed maximum speed, extra flat width has little performance value (pp. 13–15). This gives an engineering reason to stop expanding the design.

**Design from intermediate performance variables.** A literature-derived entry-speed threshold of 11.06 m/s sets platform height through gravitational energy (p. 10). A takeoff-speed/angle relation then yields a vertical component, and maximizing that component supplies the reported transition angle (pp. 11–12). An empirical acceleration trace is simplified into a one-second declining acceleration profile, which is integrated to obtain a recovery distance and flat width (pp. 13–15). A four-step calculation links takeoff speed, flight duration, ground distance, and the assumed seven tricks to total course length (pp. 15–16). These steps make the proposed geometry traceable to intermediate quantities rather than appearing as unexplained dimensions.

**Separate objectives before compromise.** The rotation model links angular velocity and flight time and produces a different transition angle from the vertical-air model (pp. 17–19). The authors then seek a judging-based tradeoff rather than choosing equal weights without explanation (p. 20). Although their mathematical implementation of the tradeoff needs correction, looking for an external performance criterion is a strong approach lesson. The scoring relationship is meant to connect engineering outputs to the actual competition objective.

**Extend a design into a usable family.** Instead of reporting only one course, the paper varies assumed entry speed from 7 to 12 m/s, tabulates corresponding dimensions, and gives small, medium, and large examples (pp. 23–24). That turns sensitivity into a design aid. It also discusses elliptical transitions and a curved bottom as alternatives, and identifies drag and rider safety as extensions (pp. 21–22, 25–26). These distinctions help an agent separate the main implemented model from exploratory alternatives and future work.

## Reported results and their conditions

For maximum vertical air, the authors report platform height 6.241 m, transition angle 51.66 degrees, radius 16.44 m, flat width 19.18 m, and course length 159.95 m (pp. 10–16). For maximum rotation, they report 45 degrees, radius 21.3 m, and length 165.96 m, retaining the same platform height and flat width (p. 19). The compromise is reported as 51.63 degrees, radius 16.37 m, and length 160.19 m (p. 20). These are outputs of the authors' assumptions and inconsistent parameter sets described below, not verified optimum dimensions.

The main assumptions are fixed sliding friction, no air resistance in the baseline, a combined rider/board entity, a common achievable maximum speed, an approximately fixed rider path, and a prescribed speed-recovery law (pp. 6, 8, 12–17). The initial assumption that vertical air is constant for an athlete also needs clarification when vertical air is subsequently the optimization target (p. 6). The model should distinguish a fixed rider capability from a shape-dependent outcome.

The course placement slope of 15–18 degrees is recommended from literature, separately from the five-degree path angle used in calculations (pp. 12, 15–16, 20–21). Keeping these different geometric angles distinct is essential; a new model should show both in one coordinate system and include longitudinal gravity consistently.

## Validation actually supplied

The paper compares its proposed length, flat width, and height with dimensions from existing courses (pp. 22–23; additional reference tables pp. 29–30). This is a useful order-of-magnitude/design-plausibility check. It does not verify the achieved vertical air, rotation angle, scoring improvement, landing safety, or optimality of the new course. Dimension definitions in the external sources also need checking, particularly whether a reported width means total course width or flat-bottom width.

The eleven-point entry-speed study gives full tables and plots of height, width, length, and transition angle (pp. 23–24, 30). It demonstrates how the design changes under one input assumption. It is not a broad robustness analysis: friction, rider power, takeoff direction, drag, and scoring uncertainty are not jointly explored. The claim that several dimensions are approximately linear is a descriptive local observation; the displayed height formula is quadratic in entry speed (pp. 10, 24).

The appendix exposes useful implementation details: one-dimensional bounded optimization for angle, a vertical-speed plot, a length calculation, and sensitivity arrays/plots (pp. 28–29). However, some sensitivity inputs are hardcoded, and the original calculation that generated them is absent. The drag extension gives an analytic work expression but no revised numerical design; safety is a proposed functional relation with no calibrated risk function or solved safe design (pp. 25–26). These should remain labeled proposals.

## Independent physical and implementation checks

1. **Check the source measurement before integrating it.** The cited body-mounted accelerometer trace contains forward/back and up/down sensor channels (p. 14). Treating a selected peak as tangential acceleration on the flat requires sensor orientation, gravity removal, and event identification. The one-second profile produces a large speed increment and is reused as a generic recovery rule (pp. 14–16). It should be validated against actual speed measurements and a rider-work budget, not inferred solely from the presence of an acceleration peak.
2. **Preserve geometric constraints during optimization.** The angle-optimization code fixes radius at 25 m and flat width at 19.81 m, whereas the final design uses radius 16.44 m and flat width 19.18 m (pp. 12, 28). Since takeoff speed depends on both radius and angle, optimizing with one radius and replacing it through a height relation afterward need not preserve the optimum. Optimize over a consistent parameterization, or iterate and demonstrate convergence. Restrict the search to a real, physically reachable takeoff-speed domain; the plotted curve includes the boundary where the square-root expression ceases to yield a feasible speed (p. 12).
3. **Retain the force mechanism that drives the design.** The initial curved-contact discussion includes the centripetal contribution, then replaces curved friction by a constant approximately equal to `mu*m*g` (pp. 7–8). No bound shows the omitted contribution is small at the proposed speed and radius. The printed normal-force equation also has a denominator `2` where a radius is expected (p. 7); the following page uses radius. Compare the full normal-force/friction model with the approximation before concluding shape differences are reliable.
4. **Do not substitute board deformation for airborne body rotation.** The compliance equations describe snowboard bending/twist along its length, but are used to motivate rider rotation; the later moment expression uses half-pipe mass times a lever arm without a force conversion (pp. 17–18). The subsequent horizontal-to-rotational velocity assumption omits the takeoff torque/angular-momentum mechanism. Its simplified angle expression drops factors involving gravity and rotational radius, and the 45-degree optimum assumes takeoff speed is independent of angle even though the earlier model makes it angle-dependent (pp. 18–19). Model angular momentum at takeoff and inertia during flight explicitly, or label the formula a limited heuristic.
5. **Optimize the score itself.** The scoring regression coefficients multiply quantities with different units—air height and rotation degrees (p. 20). Normalizing raw coefficients into weights 0.997 and 0.003 does not establish relative importance; their magnitudes change when units change. A weighted average of separately optimal angles is also not generally the optimum of a weighted performance function. Evaluate predicted height and rotation for each feasible geometry, then optimize the stated score or present a Pareto frontier.
6. **Audit body-to-code and sensitivity consistency.** The main length formula uses flat distance plus transition distance, while the appendix's `CD` already includes transitions before another transition term is added (pp. 15–16, 28). The sensitivity code fixes a radius while height and angle vary (p. 29). Its listed flat widths exceed the listed recovery distances even though the body defines width as recovery distance times cosine (pp. 15, 30). The drag expression changes the sign of the quadratic velocity term from the earlier recovery formula (pp. 15, 26). These are traceable reasons to rebuild one authoritative parameter/geometry function before any reproduction attempt.

## Transfer into the agent working document

- Define a manageable geometry family and connect each free parameter to a physical requirement or constraint.
- Decompose repeated activity into states and transitions—recovery, ascent, takeoff, flight, landing—before optimizing the whole course.
- Translate empirical data into a model only after documenting what the sensor or study measured and what transformation is required.
- Solve distinct objectives separately to reveal the conflict, then use a meaningful score or Pareto comparison; do not average parameter optima by default.
- Promote sensitivity results into a design table only after every geometry satisfies the same constraints and each operating point is feasible.
- Label checks precisely: matching existing dimensions is plausibility evidence; matching measured performance is validation; proving or certifying an optimum is a different task.
- Maintain explicit status for refinements: derived formula, implemented model, rerun result, and validated recommendation. Safety cannot be claimed from an uncalibrated extension paragraph.
