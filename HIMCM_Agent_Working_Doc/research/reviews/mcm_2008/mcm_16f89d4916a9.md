## Approach and its rationale

This paper builds a linked causal pipeline: emissions scenario → global temperature → Greenland mass balance plus ocean thermal expansion → locally adjusted sea level → connected inundation → population exposure → possible protective action. The flowchart on p. 6 is an effective map of these dependencies. It helps distinguish the climate forcing, physical response, geographic exposure, and decision layers instead of treating a sea-level forecast as the complete answer.

The team runs an educational global climate model under three controlled emissions forcings, citing a roughly ten-hour run for fifty simulated years as a feasible computational choice (p. 7). It converts global temperature change to a Greenland change by a factor of 2.2, approximates the ice sheet as a homogeneous rectangular slab, and updates accumulation, sublimation, and melting monthly. Melting is inferred from a zero-temperature location obtained with a truncated Fourier solution and `fzero`. Sea-level contributions are combined with an empirical thermal-expansion relation and a local trend correction intended to avoid double-counting global historical change (pp. 8–15).

For impacts, the team constructs a Florida-area elevation raster, uses finer elevation data selectively in sensitive low areas, and floods below-threshold pixels only if connected to the sea. It maps city/metro populations to representative points and labels the population associated with a flooded point as displaced (pp. 15–17). This is a coarse exposure proxy, not an evacuation model or a spatially resolved count of flooded residents.

## Positive patterns worth adopting

1. **Choose resolution according to the decision.** A national/global physical model and a local inundation calculation require different data. Selectively refining low coastal cells is an attempt to spend data-processing effort where a threshold decision is sensitive, rather than increase resolution uniformly (pp. 15–16).
2. **Use a controlled scenario family.** Changing emissions while keeping other forcing settings fixed makes comparisons interpretable within the chosen climate model (p. 7). Scenario labels should remain conditional scenarios, not probabilities or guaranteed extremes.
3. **Use physical accounting to justify exclusions.** The floating-ice displacement calculation attempts an order-of-magnitude check before neglecting a contribution (pp. 2–3). That is a good general method even though particular units and excluded processes need more scrutiny here.
4. **Add the missing mechanism between elevation and flooding.** Requiring connection to an ocean avoids declaring every low inland basin submerged. This explicit topological mechanism is a better starting point than elevation thresholding alone (p. 16).
5. **Test limiting cases before interpreting normal outputs.** The 0 m, 10 m, and 100 m maps test qualitatively expected behavior (pp. 16–17). These are useful implementation plausibility tests, while remaining distinct from validation at the sub-meter forecast scale.
6. **Include the computation in the evidence trail.** Appendices expose constants, input names, loop structure, raster preprocessing, flood traversal, and population assignments. This makes substantive auditing possible; it does not itself guarantee that the program implements the equations correctly.
7. **Connect a forecast to an action.** The sand-barrier calculation gives dimensions and a material-price estimate (p. 26), illustrating how a forecast can inform an explicit candidate intervention. A complete decision analysis would then test protection performance, full lifecycle cost, and alternatives.

## What the reported evidence establishes

The reported fifty-year rises are 46.92 cm, 36.61 cm, and 32.08 cm for high, intermediate, and low forcing (p. 18). Under the point-population mapping, the high case floods the representative points for Key Largo, Miami Beach, and Merritt Island by the final horizon, with a table total of about 135,000 people and 9,970 square kilometers (pp. 19–20). These are conditional model outputs, not independently reproduced forecasts.

The three forcing trajectories show scenario sensitivity. The extreme inundation maps demonstrate broad plausibility of the raster routine. A statement that forecast levels are within 10 cm of other institutions' models is a cross-model consistency claim (p. 23); the PDF supplies neither a detailed benchmark table nor a historical hindcast with residuals. Its stronger claims that past data have been accurately fitted and future predictions are accurate (pp. 4, 26) therefore outrun the visible validation evidence. There is no demonstrated uncertainty propagation from emissions, climate response, physical parameters, elevation error, or population representation to the final displacement figures.

## Mathematical checks that matter before implementation

**Unit and sign consistency.** The ice volume/area ratio on p. 9 is printed as 1498 km, while the numbers imply approximately 1.498 km; the code correctly initializes 1498 m. “Mass balance” is explicitly a volume in the later equations, requiring density conversion (p. 13). Equation 23 defines positive balance as accumulation minus loss, but equation 24 multiplies that balance directly into a positive sea-level contribution: the loss-to-sea sign should be made explicit. The appendix instead uses decreasing ice height to generate positive sea-level rise. These differences show why every module interface needs signed units and a worked example.

**Boundary conditions and the heat equation must agree.** The paper calls its conditions Neumann, writes homogeneous zero-value endpoint conditions for the residual, and then uses a cosine series with a constant mode (pp. 11–12). Those are not interchangeable boundary specifications. The diffusivity in the PDE is absent from the displayed exponential decay term; the stated initial residual is not consistently carried through the coefficient calculation. A moving thickness and time-varying boundary temperature also require explaining how the steady-state subtraction is updated. Solving for the position where temperature reaches zero is not by itself a latent-heat balance for melting. These are structural derivation gaps, not merely parameter uncertainty.

**A spatial mean can hide the process that dominates a threshold.** The uniform slab suppresses elevation/temperature heterogeneity, seasonal melt zones, and dynamic loss processes. Evidence about Antarctic sea ice is not automatically evidence about the land-based Antarctic ice sheet; the exclusion argument on p. 3 needs that distinction. The paper acknowledges simplified physics, but a sensitivity or bounding calculation is still needed to show that the simplification is fit for the requested horizon.

**Resolution and accuracy are separate.** The text reports an elevation error of about ±4.3 ft for Florida NED data and forecasts rises of only about 0.3–0.5 m (pp. 15, 18). A finer raster or more decimal places does not remove vertical error or datum uncertainty. Exact dates at which a city-point crosses a threshold are therefore especially fragile. Population concentration at representative points creates artificial jumps; metro and individual-city entries also need a disjoint geographic accounting scheme before summing displaced people.

## What the code inspection adds

The following observations refer to the printed appendix, not a verified execution of an original repository:

- **Thirteen monthly steps are treated as one year.** Each scenario increments `mcount`, records at `mcount == 13`, then resets to zero (pp. 30–32). This advances the physical ice process for thirteen months per output “year.” A calendar-based time index and a constant-forcing regression test are needed.
- **The thermal-expansion input changes meaning.** Global temperatures are transformed to Greenland temperatures and a synthetic seasonal cycle of amplitude 15°C is added (p. 29). The later expansion update uses changes in that Greenland monthly series while labeling them global annual temperature change, and does not display the diffusivity factor from equation 25 (pp. 30–32). Separate global and regional forcing arrays would prevent this interface error.
- **The series is not coded as the printed termwise sum.** The `U` function includes an unexplained `1e-2` multiplying the steady-state gradient. Its Fourier expression uses `/ (n.*pi).^2` rather than elementwise `./`, so row-vector matrix division replaces the intended elementwise division before summation (p. 32, visually checked). Independent scalar-loop versus vectorized-function comparisons would catch this immediately.
- **The raster neighbor rule checks diagonals.** `nindX=[X-1 X+1]` and `nindY=[Y-1 Y+1]` followed by `datagrid2(nindX,nindY)` selects the four diagonal neighbors, not the usual four edge-sharing or eight surrounding neighbors (p. 35). The model needs an explicit adjacency rule and tiny channel/barrier test maps. The useful conceptual connectivity requirement is not enough if its implementation changes the reachable set.
- **Reproduction needs more than the PDF.** The scripts refer to three external temperature files, geographic datasets, a saved `hiresworkspace`, and manually fetched refinement outputs. The refinement-generation/import blocks are commented while subsequent code uses their variables (pp. 33–34). The inundation script has a fixed `SLR=5` example value rather than an included driver for the reported decade/scenario table (p. 35). A clean-session run with a dependency manifest is required before claiming reproducibility.
- **Spatial metadata must follow array transformations.** The raster is flipped vertically while city latitude arrays descend from north to south (pp. 33, 35–36). Correct correspondence should be demonstrated using known landmarks and the returned referencing metadata, rather than assumed from the plotted image. Likewise, land-area totals need an explicit geographic mask and latitude-aware cell areas; the displayed grid includes areas outside Florida.

## Claim and recommendation audit

The result discussion says Miami Beach is delayed twenty years in the medium case relative to the high case (p. 22), while the tables show its first reported inundation at year 30 versus year 40: one decade. The final discussion rounds the high-case displacement to approximately 200,000, although its table is approximately 135,000 (pp. 20, 24). Table 4's caption repeats the intermediate-scenario label although its surrounding text describes the low scenario (p. 23). These are direct examples of why summary claims should be generated or checked against a single result table.

The proposed 0.5 m sand structure is said to provide at least fifty years of safety (p. 26). A mean-sea-level forecast alone cannot justify this protection claim: surge, waves, erosion, drainage, breaches, and maintenance are absent from the model. The paper itself notes unmodeled erosion, saltwater intrusion, and economic effects (p. 24). Its material-volume estimate should therefore be labeled an initial construction-cost illustration, not a demonstrated cost-benefit result or a guaranteed protection design.

The reference page in this local PDF is blank, and several in-text citations appear as empty gaps. This may be a damaged/exported local artifact; it should not be attributed confidently to the team's submitted version. It does prevent this copy from supporting a complete source audit. Preserve that provenance limit instead of fabricating a bibliography.

- **Domain/data role:** establish land ice versus floating ice, input scenario provenance, elevation vertical datum and error, geographic masks, and disjoint population support.
- **Modeler:** justify the simplest physically consistent baseline, specify boundary/initial conditions, and use conservative bounds or alternative structures for omitted processes.
- **Computation role:** verify calendar stepping, mass conservation and signs, scalar/vector equation agreement, raster adjacency, and landmark coordinates; package the actual scenario driver and input dependencies.
- **Independent reviewer:** compare structural and measurement uncertainty with the decision threshold, audit every headline against its result table, and keep limiting-case tests separate from historical validation.
- **Writer/visuals role:** provide inset maps at the vulnerable coastline, uncertainty bands or threshold ranges, traceable conditional scenario labels, and a recommendation strength that matches the modeled hazards.

The reusable contribution is the staged causal architecture and explicit geographic mechanism. Reliable transfer requires the numerical, physical, and spatial interfaces to survive independent checks before its attractive end-to-end narrative is trusted.
