## The modeling decision that drives the paper

The authors first distinguish solving a puzzle from predicting how hard it is for a person to solve. An exact-cover solver can establish feasibility very quickly without representing a human's cognitive effort. They therefore define the target as the expected solving time of a hypothetical expert and operationalize it as the number of candidate strategy applications that the expert checks (pp. 5–8). This is a particularly useful approach pattern: define the latent quantity and target population before borrowing a convenient computational score.

Their hypothetical expert knows a six-tier strategy library, searches easier tiers first, examines candidate applications in random order, and performs each check at approximately equal cost. Given `p` possible applications and `v` valid ones in a tier, the expected position of the first success is `(p+1)/(v+1)` when `v>0`. The paper derives this order-statistic result using a tail-sum argument and a binomial identity (p. 9). The program counts possible and valid applications, adds expected search cost, randomly chooses a valid move, updates the candidate graph, and repeats. Twenty runs average over different solution paths.

The mechanism produces a board-dependent cost for a strategy without assigning a fixed difficulty weight to every named technique. It still depends on the chosen tier order, equal check cost, strategy vocabulary, and random-search assumption. Thus it reduces one form of arbitrary weighting; it does not produce a human-independent definition of difficulty.

## Why the architecture is useful

**The rationale follows the target rather than technique popularity.** The authors discuss clue-count heuristics, exact-cover methods, prior algorithmic ratings, and human-style deduction before selecting their process model (pp. 5–7). The useful inference is that a weak global heuristic need not explain an individual puzzle's difficulty. Their claim that heuristics cannot be useful for a specific puzzle is stronger than necessary; a learned predictive baseline could still be informative and should be compared empirically.

**An analytical subproblem replaces unnecessary random work.** The program samples the move trajectory but uses an exact conditional expectation for within-tier search (pp. 8–9). This separates uncertainty that matters to later board states from randomness that can be integrated out. The working document can ask agents to identify such tractable subproblems before writing a large Monte Carlo loop.

**Scoring and construction share a clear interface.** Both generators produce a valid candidate, invoke the same difficulty evaluator, and accept the candidate only if its score lies in the requested interval (pp. 12–17). The metric is an evaluator; the generator is a proposal mechanism; uniqueness is a separate feasibility property. Keeping these layers separate makes each testable.

**A failure mode motivates the second generator.** Random construction overwhelmingly produces easy puzzles, so rejection sampling becomes expensive for hard target intervals. The pseudo-generator preserves a hard residual structure from an existing seed while varying easier clues. The authors explicitly disclose that this trades structural diversity for difficulty and speed, instead of presenting transformed seeds as wholly new random solutions (pp. 14–18).

**Validation is planned along multiple axes.** The paper separately evaluates agreement with another rating system, distributional shape, computational time, unique solvability, and generation yield. This is a stronger structure than reporting a few attractive example puzzles, even though several individual checks need qualification.

## Generator logic and its guarantees

The standard generator repeatedly inserts a random candidate into an empty cell, applies sound deductions, and restarts after a contradiction. Its final clues are the manually inserted digits; digits established by deduction are removed. A deduction sequence from those clues certifies the resulting solution, provided all inference rules are sound. The paper specifically changes backtracking so that a successful speculative completion does not count as a forced deduction: only a contradiction can eliminate the guessed alternative (p. 13). This is an excellent example of modifying a solver when its role changes from finding one solution to certifying uniqueness.

The pseudo-generator first solves a seed with lower-tier rules until stuck and reserves the remaining cells. It inserts only values from the completed seed in unreserved cells and uses lower-tier deductions until those cells are filled (pp. 13–14). The new clues imply the original unreserved configuration; a uniquely solvable seed can therefore support a uniqueness argument. However, “requires tier k+1” is relative to this solver's rule set and search protocol, not a theorem about every possible human strategy. Each generated puzzle should be rechecked for validity, uniqueness, achieved difficulty, and lower-tier failure.

The text also describes randomly permuting rows and columns (p. 15). To preserve Sudoku, these must be valid grid symmetries, such as rows within a band and permutations of whole bands, rather than arbitrary individual-row permutations across bands. A digit relabeling preserves validity. An implementation should encode these symmetry constraints explicitly and distinguish symmetry-equivalent outputs from genuinely different structures.

## Evidence and its limits

The rating comparison uses 800 puzzles, 100 from each of eight external categories, runs the model twenty times per puzzle, and then divides its scores into eight equally sized groups (p. 10). The table visibly concentrates toward its diagonal, particularly at the easy end, and the paper reports Goodman–Kruskal gamma 0.82. This supports ordinal association with that source's labeling under that sample design. It does not establish calibrated solving times, agreement for all skill levels, or superiority over competing metrics. Gamma excludes tied pairs; its value should be interpreted together with category overlap and a tie-aware alternative.

The printed chi-square value 6350 needs correction or explanation. For an 8-by-8 table with total 800, Pearson chi-square cannot exceed `800 × 7 = 5600`; this is an analytical consistency check, not a recomputation of the reported statistic. A result may still strongly reject independence, but statistical significance does not validate the exact score scale or every category assignment. The paper's dismissal of more precise error analysis is particularly weak when the generator makes decisions near score thresholds.

The 1000-puzzle score distribution is compared with a web image of solving times for **Easy** puzzles across many users (pp. 10–11). Both are right-skewed, but the samples differ in puzzle composition, solver population, and measurement units. Matching marginal shapes does not establish that the model ranks the same individual puzzles correctly or predicts human time. The millions of observations behind the external curve do not compensate for that mismatch. A matched puzzle-by-solver dataset would test the stated construct directly.

The paper reports 7.8 seconds to rate a puzzle with twenty iterations on specified historical hardware (p. 12). It uses approximately 0.15 minutes per generated-and-rated valid board and estimates target-generation time by a geometric waiting model: cost per attempt divided by the estimated hit probability (p. 16). The reported four-level times are 0.25, 3.1, 4.7, and 30 minutes. This is an informative deployment metric: users care how long it takes to receive a puzzle at the desired difficulty, not merely how fast any valid board is created.

There are qualifications. The four reported interval counts, 598, 49, 32, and 5, total 684 out of 1000 trials (pp. 15–16). The remaining 316 need an explicit category, such as below the sampled score range, rather than disappearing from the accounting. The timed experiment includes 100 valid and 30 invalid boards, while the yield denominator must identify whether it counts valid outputs or all construction attempts. Independent identical trials and sufficiently stable cost are assumptions behind the geometric estimate, and just five hard hits leave the extreme runtime estimate uncertain. Repeated rating can also move a candidate across a boundary; the reported ratio of standard deviation to mean near 0.1 needs a clear distinction between per-run variability and uncertainty of the twenty-run average (p. 9).

The pseudo-generator example uses one seed scored 8122 and twenty derived puzzles averaging 7111, produced in three minutes (pp. 16–17). It is a successful reported demonstration for that seed. The authors appropriately admit they lack a large enough seed database to estimate target-bin hit probabilities. It should not be generalized into a universal runtime or diversity guarantee.

## Boundary cases the workflow should require

- **Zero valid applications:** the first-success formula assumes `v>0`. If `v=0`, the search exhausts `p` checks and escalates to another tier; `(p+1)/(v+1)` must not be applied as though success occurred. The PDF does not expose code to check this branch.
- **No-progress moves:** a recognized pattern must actually remove a candidate or fill a cell. Otherwise repeated selection can inflate cost or fail to terminate.
- **Unsupported puzzles:** if all implemented tiers fail, the evaluator must return an explicit unresolved status rather than silently giving the difficulty of a partial solution. Limited two-candidate, singles-only backtracking is not a complete solver (pp. 23–24).
- **Check costs:** a naked-single check and a hidden-quad or backtracking check can be constant time on a fixed board without having equal human duration. Equal asymptotic order does not justify equal cognitive cost.
- **Sampling target:** percentiles of a convenience collection are not percentiles of all mathematical Sudoku boards or all puzzles sold to users. Freeze and version the reference population for difficulty bands; the text also cites different collection references in its distribution descriptions (pp. 10, 15).
- **Novelty:** seeded outputs, symmetric equivalents, and independently constructed boards should have separate diversity measures. A large output count does not establish structural diversity.

## Role-specific lessons for the working document

The **coordinator** should define the user population and acceptance criteria before modeling. The **domain/data role** should gather matched human-time observations or clearly label external ratings as proxies, document sampling and skill level, and reserve a validation set. The **modeler** should state the strategy library, search order, check-cost assumptions, and edge cases of the expectation formula. The **computation role** should produce deduction traces, independently count solutions, log failures and all generation outcomes, and measure target-level latency with uncertainty. The **independent reviewer** should distinguish sound deductions from successful guesses, test symmetries and unsupported cases, audit statistics from the displayed table, and challenge distribution-only validation. The **writer/visuals role** should present the score as a model of a specified solver, report seed diversity and benchmark scope, and disclose missing source artifacts.

The transferable success is the sequence **define the construct → model a mechanism → derive a computable score → validate against external evidence → generate under that score → explain feasibility, speed, and diversity separately**. Its assumptions remain part of the answer rather than disappearing behind the word “objective.”
