## Demand map and architecture

The official problem requests consumption trends, costs and payment, venue differences, modeled interventions and a school-newspaper article. The paper explicitly maps those questions to sections on pp.4–5. Its article is p.2; demand models occupy pp.7–16; results pp.17–20; initiatives pp.21–23. Installation costs, rebates, taxpayers and commercial benefits are discussed in the introduction, but the numerical cost function includes only purchased electricity. Thus the delivered cost estimate is narrower than the broader economic-cost framing.

The personal-device branch is `annual venue attendance and normalized intraday arrivals → device-owning visitors → finite outlet/non-outlet seats → battery and dwell-time updates → daily kWh per venue → location aggregation × electricity tariff`. An EV branch models repeated 30%-battery charging sessions subject to a 24-hour time budget and scales by estimated outlets. This is a useful example of combining different model types through an energy-and-cost interface rather than forcing all mechanisms into one formula.

The NetLogo state includes each visitor's devices, remaining batteries, drainage and charge rates, seat assignment and remaining stay. A minute advances all batteries; the lowest-battery device at an outlet is charged; a visitor leaves when time expires or any device reaches zero. Visitors with no devices leave as “flybys”; visitors finding no seat leave immediately (pp.8–9, 47–52). There is no waiting queue, movement to a newly freed outlet or group seating mechanism.

## Author selection rationale

The authors explicitly choose an agent-based model because individual behavior, nonuniform arrivals and finite charging availability interact locally, while aggregate price and attendance change over years (p.5). **Reviewer inference:** the strongest justification is resource competition and heterogeneous device states, not randomness alone; simpler event simulation or queueing models could also represent random arrivals. The paper does not compare their complexity or predictive performance.

EVs receive a separate analytical/computational model because their charging powers, battery capacities and station access differ from small devices (pp.15–16). Commercial prices use a logarithmic time trend, justified by diminishing infrastructure-driven increases. The mechanism is asserted rather than estimated, and no alternative price forecast is evaluated (pp.7–8). EV count and vehicle/outlet ratio use quadratic fits selected from multiple regressions, with in-sample R² of 0.9947 and 0.9776; the alternative fits and a held-out forecast test are not shown (p.16).

## Data, behavioral assumptions and parameterization

Five destination types are modeled: airports, cafés, Amtrak stations, public libraries and public secondary/postsecondary schools. Other destinations are excluded as negligible. Assumptions hold venue counts, device ownership and efficiency fixed over a decade, using small annual changes or recent saturation as justification (pp.6–11). Several individually small omissions can accumulate; the paper acknowledges this. Population growth is dismissed partly because children do not own devices, but cohorts age during a ten-year forecast.

Adult phone/laptop/tablet ownership probabilities are approximately 0.77/0.73/0.53 and are independently sampled in the code. Applying adult ownership to school populations and treating device ownership as the reason for sitting down are additional behavioral assumptions. The latter makes almost every visitor a potential charging customer, since the independent probability of owning none is small. The authors acknowledge likely overestimation from actual café flybys (p.21).

Initial battery is uniform on 50–100% before noon and 25–75% after noon. All owned devices drain throughout the visit; one device charges at a time. Battery charging is assumed linear using a voltage/charge discussion, although linear voltage behavior does not by itself establish constant state-of-charge gain over time (pp.10–11). Dwell ranges and venue capacities are listed, with one seat per 36 square feet as a fallback (pp.11, 15).

Google Popular Times profiles are fitted with quartic/quintic polynomials and normalized into arrival densities (pp.12–14). Relative occupancy/popularity is not automatically arrival flow: a long stay can create a high occupancy reading without many new arrivals. The polynomial also needs a nonnegativity check. The displayed airport coefficient `4143x`, interval-count expression and half-hour normalization need reconciliation before reuse. School arrival times vary between 7–7:30, 7:30–8 and 8–8:30 across the paper (pp.10, 13).

The appendices make geographic and unit issues visible. A table labeled US airports includes Toronto, Vancouver, Sydney, Melbourne and other non-US entries; its “terminal” counts are later interpreted as gates (pp.32–33, 46). The Amtrak capacity derivation calls platform area square metres and divides by a square-feet-per-seat rule, while the main table labels the same area square feet (pp.11, 34–43, 46). Several derivation citations remain placeholders. These are reasons to validate the data dictionary before running more simulations.

## Simulation results and validation strength

The authors report 100 NetLogo repetitions per year and outlet-density combination and plot mean daily energy. They find saturation in railway/library cases, an airport/café plateau at higher outlet densities, and continued school sensitivity (pp.17–19). This gives a useful conditional distinction: extra capacity may not change consumption once demand is served. The result does not establish that **every** visitor is guaranteed an outlet at the reported threshold. That claim needs direct unmet-demand or service-probability measurements, including peak scenarios. Similar mean energy at two settings is insufficient.

There is no meter-data validation of the device simulation. Repeating a behavioral model reduces Monte Carlo noise but does not verify its assumptions. Some plots show error bars, without clearly defining their statistical meaning; the full distribution and seeds are not supplied. A zero-outlet outcome of zero energy is a useful structural check, though it cannot validate realistic charging behavior.

For EVs, the 2018 model estimate of 0.618 TWh is compared with 20% of a 2.85-TWh total, yielding 0.57 TWh and an absolute discrepancy of 0.048 TWh (p.20). This is about 8.4% relative to the constructed benchmark, not a direct observation of free public charging. “More than 80% at home” does not identify an exact 20% public/free fraction, and workplace charging scope is unresolved. One base-year match does not validate a ten-year forecast.

## Aggregation and code audit

The most consequential discrepancy is the day-to-year conversion. The text says daily venue kWh is multiplied by 365 (p.18), but the appendix cost values match **daily** national aggregation. For example, library energy 8.109 kWh × $0.1067/kWh × 9,057 libraries gives approximately the reported $7,836.7, without a yearly multiplier (pp.31, 44). The school and airport tables show the same pattern. The individual-location plot also shows roughly $10/day for a low-density airport while the prose calls it annual cost (p.19). Consequently the summary's national annual personal-device cost estimates are not supported by the displayed calculations. Correcting the calendar requires actual operating days, especially for schools, rather than automatically using 365.

Other concrete implementation discrepancies include:

- “Public outlet density” is initially defined as square feet per outlet, then used as outlets per square foot. The stated upper bound of 1/36 and code multiplication by area use the latter (pp.11–12, 48).
- Café capacity is described as 50–70, but the provided 1,000-square-foot code computes approximately 28 seats. The dwell expression `random 67 + random 141` permits zero minutes and is not a uniform draw between 1.12 and 3.4 hours (pp.11, 15, 48, 50).
- The repeat-run procedure does not show a full world clear before rebuilding, and it contains a literal LaTeX `\usepackage{listings}` fragment. Long code lines are clipped; the exact charge-accounting conversion cannot be reconstructed from this PDF (pp.48–52).
- Only the café NetLogo configuration is supplied. Other venue parameter sets, exported runs and graph-generation code are absent.

The EV code repeatedly samples charger power for sequential sessions within one time budget, then scales by a national outlet estimate (p.53). Fixed stations do not change their power category each session, so this is not obviously equivalent to summing throughput across a fixed charger mix. Full occupancy is assumed, ignoring utilization and arrival limits. The declared public-share variable is not visibly used in the untruncated code; the right end of the energy line is missing. The model therefore needs a demand-versus-capacity reconciliation before its estimate is interpreted as actual consumption.

## Recommendations, writing and workflow transfer

The interventions are well connected to model inputs: restrict outlet availability or change battery drain/charge parameters (pp.21–22). The observation that removing surplus outlets may leave electricity usage unchanged is useful. However, reducing served demand is not automatically an efficient social policy; a service-level requirement or lost-convenience cost is needed. Greater battery capacity and lower energy use are also different changes, so technology claims need a physical energy balance.

The paper's demand-to-section map, state-transition diagram, destination-specific input tables and distinction between national and per-location results are strong communication devices. Unresolved citations, figure references and source-code clipping show the need for a separate publication audit.

Transfer this into role contracts: the **simulation engineer** owns explicit states, transitions, random seeds and per-run exports; the **data steward** distinguishes arrivals from occupancy and keeps units/geography consistent; the **validator** checks charging-energy conservation, unmet demand, multiple runs and held-out observations; the **integration lead** checks every scale conversion from one agent to one venue to one day to the national year. The paper supports the hybrid architecture as a candidate approach, while its precise cost and capacity guarantees require correction and independent validation.
