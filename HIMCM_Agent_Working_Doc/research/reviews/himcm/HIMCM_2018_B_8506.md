## Demand map and complete architecture

The paired official two-page problem requires automatic response to departures and anticipation of arrivals despite irregular schedules; personal and regional conditions; a single-zone algorithm; benefits/issues and comparison with at least two existing products; a multi-zone extension; and a one-page nontechnical news release. The paper addresses these through the single-zone model pp.6–21, Nest/Ecobee comparison pp.21–24, multi-zone extension pp.24–28, and news release p.3. It does not fully achieve the strongest irregular-schedule requirement: sufficiently irregular users are asked to enter their return time manually (p.21). Air pollution is discussed as a limitation rather than controlled (p.24).

The model chain is:

`Thermostat history → period detection → phase-aligned weighted preference prediction → Gaussian comfort utility + coupled room/wall heat dynamics → discretized temperature sequence search → humidity/allergen restrictions and arrival-dependent comfort → manual-update feedback.`

For multiple zones, the authors replace detailed physical measurement with calibration of a heat-transfer graph, use a transition matrix to predict unconditioned temperatures, calculate energy needed to reach selected temperatures, and combine occupants' comfort functions by multiplication (pp.25–28). This is a genuine interface between prediction, dynamics, optimization and human preferences; the interfaces are also where several inconsistencies occur.

## Explicit selection rationale versus reviewer inference

Period detection is explicitly motivated by examples of shift workers whose cycles differ from the conventional weekday/weekend schedule. Autocorrelation identifies repeating structure; selecting the shortest lag above 0.7 is justified by the greater relevance of recent observations (pp.7–8). Historical weights depend on closeness to the most recent corresponding observation, with corrections for monotone drift and recent weather shocks (pp.8–10). These are designed heuristics, not statistically estimated optimal predictors.

The thermal model separates air/furniture from concrete after a heat-capacity calculation shows that concrete dominates (pp.13–14). This is a strong example of using an order-of-magnitude calculation to change model structure. The authors discuss solar/thermal radiation, quantify its potentially large scale, and omit it because of measurement and computational complexity (pp.15–16). The omission is disclosed, but the size estimate weakens any claim that its effect is negligible.

A nonsmooth power function motivates discretization; an explicit count of 39^96 or 9^96 possible schedules rules out brute force. MCTS is then motivated by sequential decisions with state-dependent future utility and large search spaces (pp.15–17). This explains why exhaustive enumeration is unsuitable, but does not establish that MCTS is preferable to dynamic programming, model predictive control or other continuous/discrete optimization methods. That stronger comparison is a **reviewer-proposed test**, not an author result.

For shared rooms, the authors give a concrete counterexample: summing two narrow comfort peaks can favor one person's preferred temperature instead of a compromise, whereas multiplying them favors the midpoint for equal widths (p.28). This is an unusually useful justification pattern: state desired decision behavior, show where an alternative violates it, then choose an aggregation rule. Interpreting normalized Gaussian density as acceptance probability still needs justification.

## Data, assumptions and weights

The design records 96 fifteen-minute blocks per day and retains about two months. One history uses -99999 for absence to detect schedule cycles; a second interpolates absent-temperature values for preference prediction (p.7). Such a sentinel dominates numerical autocorrelation and conflates occupancy periodicity with temperature periodicity. Preference is inferred from actual settings under the assumption that uncomfortable occupants intervene; silence therefore becomes implicit approval (p.5).

User tolerance controls Gaussian variance from 0.5 to 3.5. Comfort and power receive coefficients alpha and negative beta, with scale adjustments because their magnitudes differ (pp.11–12, 16). The paper's energy calculations assume a small 30-square-metre apartment, concrete thermal mass, constant heat-pump COP and simplified conduction. Multi-zone calibration requires deliberately holding neighboring zones at fixed temperatures while stopping the target unit (pp.25–27); feasibility, parameter identifiability and noise are not demonstrated.

The available tables are synthetic student and retired-person schedules with missing periods, gradual drift and abrupt changes (pp.43–67). They are examples, not measured household logs, and only part of the full synthetic dataset is supplied. Competitor descriptions and savings figures are historical claims from the cited 2018-era sources; they should not be reused as current product specifications.

## Validation and bounded conclusions

Synthetic preference scenarios report mean absolute errors 0.28, 0.57, 0.67, 0.99, 0.91 and 1.01 degrees, with anomaly maxima up to 4.92 degrees (p.30). Thus the abstract's consistently-below-one-degree statement is too broad, and the release's within-one-degree phrasing obscures large individual errors. The tests do demonstrate a useful scenario structure: gradual drift, tapering and abnormalities of multiple lengths, with reaction time as an operational metric. They do not establish real-home performance.

The period-threshold plot relates autocorrelation to the largest empirical return-time mass, rather than measuring calibrated return predictions on held-out observations. The authors admit too few cases between 0.6 and 0.7 and adopt 0.7 conservatively (pp.28–29). This is a disclosed judgment, not a statistically established universal threshold.

Ten MCTS runs are said to save 14.6% with standard error 2.58% under one setup (p.17). Climate/weight scenarios report approximately 9.1–38.2% savings and small uncertainty figures (p.31). The comparisons are theoretical and do not share a field-test protocol with the competitor data. Runtime and random-search variability are admitted. The appendix does not supply the promised MCTS raw-run table or a complete reproducible experiment configuration. No hardware, multi-zone calibration or user-comfort validation is provided.

## Equation, implementation and claim audit

- The stored two months cannot generally provide five previous periods when periods may last 31 days; the prediction appendix reshapes six periods by default (pp.7–10, 40). Cold-start and long-period history availability require a coherent rule.
- Equation 35 prints saturated vapor density as R(T+273)/(PM), while its following equality multiplies by the vapor-pressure expression. Both the dimensional relationship and displayed Buck-expression placement need correction before implementation (p.18). The allergen table even lists asthma as an allergen. These are author claims to audit, not health guidance.
- The return section rejects a CDF and uses probability of returning in a particular block as probability of needing comfort, then sets every later block to one (pp.20–21). A return-time mass is not occupancy probability. The Ruby appendix instead explicitly computes and uses a CDF (pp.35–36). This changes the policy, and the CDF loop includes the array length as an index.
- The text says turn the system off after a 15-minute absence (p.20), while the comparison describes continued operation at changing power (p.23). The actual policy needs one definition.
- The visible Ruby utility method returns only alpha times satisfaction; beta and the power term are omitted (p.35). Consequently the supplied search objective does not implement equation 32. The cost is accumulated separately after selection (p.39). Several code lines are clipped in the source PDF, further limiting reproducibility.
- The appendix uses wall capacity 3,000,000, alpha 500, beta -2, 0.125-degree actions and 12,000 simulations (pp.35–38); these differ from the main illustrative setup and reported experiments. A frozen parameter/run manifest is missing.
- A product of Gaussian functions is proportional to a Gaussian even with unequal variances; the assertion that unequal widths make the product skewed is incorrect (p.28). The useful compromise argument survives without that claim.

## Transfer to an agent workflow

The strongest transferable practices are the heat-capacity argument for adding a state, the decision-space count for rejecting brute force, the shared-comfort counterexample, and scenario tests chosen for distinct behavioral failures. The news release translates technical features clearly but overstates generality and precision; its claims should be generated from an evidence ledger.

Assign a dynamics agent to physical units, state and actuator limits; a prediction agent to occupancy versus preference data and time-respecting evaluation; a controller agent to optimization and baselines; and a validator to uncertainty, policy consistency and objective/code agreement. The integrator must demand an explicit interface contract: what is observed, what is predicted, what is a controllable setpoint, what is a simulated actual temperature, and what quantity the search maximizes. This role structure is **reviewer inference**, derived from the paper's concrete seams rather than asserted by its authors.
