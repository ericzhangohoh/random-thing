## Problem demand and response

The official 2017 B statement requires a physically located ski-area design with at least 160 km of slopes, approximately 20/40/40 beginner/intermediate/difficult distribution, comparison with existing North American resorts, and a two-page client memo. It explicitly distinguishes percent grade from degrees and notes that a trail is rated by its most difficult part. The paper supplies a two-page memo (pp.2–3), a trail-quality construction and candidate-generation process (pp.6–12), a resort evaluation system (pp.12–19), and mapped/ranked results (pp.20–25). It additionally defines an Olympic-readiness score. A distinction between *number of trails*, *total length*, *spatially mapped trails*, and *simulated trail attributes* is essential to interpreting coverage.

## Architecture and module interfaces

`Mapped terrain and candidate paths → trail attributes → skill-conditioned Trail Quality Index (TQI) → accepted trail collection and simulated extensions → resort characteristics → weighted resort and Olympic-readiness scores → client memo.`

The two main models are described as independent but the designed resort's attributes feed the ranking model (p.6). This is a useful explicit interface: one module constructs a system and another evaluates it against external alternatives. TQI combines average gradient, roughness, turn sharpness, width, flatness, uphill/energy-loss proportion and gradient IQR (pp.6–9). Exponents change preference direction by skier level. The score is logarithmically compressed for readability. The ranking model combines snow quality, lift provision, trail density and fit to three skill groups (pp.13–19); a changed skill mixture creates the Olympic variant (pp.21–22).

## Reported approach and justification

**Author-reported:** each TQI factor is introduced by explaining what it means to different skiers. IQR is chosen for resistance to outliers (p.8); its square-root contribution reflects an explicitly stated lower importance (p.9). Logistic transforms keep selected component scores bounded; adjustable parameters create differentiation among resorts (pp.14–16). The 20/40/40 mix comes from the requested slope distribution (p.13). Snow aspect is supported by a cited weather explanation (p.14). A geographically large search problem motivates generation and screening of candidate paths rather than a global geometric optimum (pp.1, 9–12).

**Reviewer inference:** the pedagogically useful idea is to build a score from interpretable monotonic relationships and explain each transformation. This does not establish that the particular multiplicative form, exponent choices, component weights or calibration constants are empirically correct. The paper does not report a comparative experiment against an additive TQI or other plausible ranking model. Using desired trail proportions as population preference weights is a value judgment that should be made explicit.

## Data, assumptions and feasibility

Google Earth/Maps paths and elevations supply terrain attributes; resort data and ranking websites provide comparison information (pp.9, 21, 26). Assumptions include removable obstacles, separate counting of merged trails, different skier preferences and hosting only part of the Olympics (pp.5–6). These assumptions materially affect feasibility and interpretation.

The authors map roughly 60 km of candidate trails, retain roughly 40 km, and then simulate remaining trail attributes using fitted normal distributions (pp.9–11). The text gives 100 km of simulated remaining trails on p.10, whereas the summary describes 120 km; the final total is more than 162 km (pp.1, 22). The p.11 flowchart confirms simulation of attributes from means and standard deviations. Such simulation can support a hypothetical scenario, but does not itself prove that the additional length fits on the actual terrain, remains connected, avoids overlaps, or meets the slope constraints. Input variables also have physical bounds and dependence that unconstrained independent Gaussian sampling may not preserve.

## Validation and claim strength

The paper compares TQI values for a fall line, generated slope and a known Whistler trail (p.10); compares resort rankings with an external website (p.23); perturbs snowfall and slope aspect (p.24); and illustrates sensitivity to a logistic calibration constant (p.25). These are useful plausibility and local response checks. They do not justify the strong phrase that the ranking is immune to sensitivity analysis or prove Olympic readiness. External rankings share related factors, so agreement is not independent ground truth. No terrain-wide coverage proof or independent validation of skier preference weights is reported.

**Static consistency checks:** the p.21 table puts four listed resorts above the proposed resort's 7.44 score, yet calls the proposed resort third; this should be reconciled before using that rank. The beginner-fit constant differs between the p.15/17 presentation and the p.25 sensitivity discussion. The final trail-acceptance threshold also differs from earlier illustrative threshold language (pp.10, 20). The trail-density expression subtracts a dimensional length/area quantity from 1 (p.18), requiring a clear reference scale to preserve unit invariance. These observations identify review questions; no corrected model has been computed here.

## Strengths of explanation and transferable workflow rules

1. Derive a composite score incrementally, explaining each factor's direction and role before presenting the final formula.
2. Separate constructing an alternative from comparing it against existing alternatives, and name the data passed between those modules.
3. State the stakeholder interpretation of weights and record which weights come from the task and which are additional judgment.
4. Track provenance at the level of observed, mapped and simulated quantities. A simulated design attribute must not silently become a verified physical design.
5. Check the abstract, memo, results table and conclusion against one authoritative result record. Rank claims need direct table checks.
6. Distinguish a useful sensitivity illustration from evidence of decision stability across plausible parameter ranges.

The memo is a clear attempt to translate a long technical analysis into a client recommendation, while explicitly acknowledging omitted non-skiing facilities (p.3). A stronger workflow would make that limitation constrain the recommendation's wording throughout.
