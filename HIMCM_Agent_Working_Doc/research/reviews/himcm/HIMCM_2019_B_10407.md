## Demand coverage and model architecture

The report organizes the problem into four stages: estimate baseline beverage consumption (pp.6–11), redistribute consumption after removal of PET water (pp.11–14), translate volume changes into impacts (pp.14–19), and recommend policy adjustments (p.20). It includes acquisition suggestions for data-rich and data-poor locations, worked airport/Concord/San Francisco applications, strengths and limitations (pp.21–22), and a one-page newspaper article (p.3). Generalization is embedded in its location-adjustment procedure rather than developed as a separate scale study. The revised policy is described without a new numerical experiment.

The interface is especially clear: `national or local beverage shares → local daily volumes → post-ban volumes → signed volume changes → three-component impact vectors → overall score`. Eight categories are tracked: bottled water, tap water, dairy, juice, coffee, tea, soda and alcohol. Every stage produces an inspectable intermediate table. This is a strong example of organizing a report around what each model receives and returns.

The implementation removes all bottled water in each location. The article correctly mentions the PET/plain-water/≤1 L target, but the equations do not distinguish permitted larger bottles, non-PET bottles or San Francisco's city-property coverage. An exact policy-to-product/location mapping is needed before interpreting a citywide intervention.

## Selection rationale: what is explicit and what is inferred

The authors explicitly argue that changed drinking patterns are the direct effect of the ban and that environmental, economic and health consequences follow from those changes (p.4). They model changes rather than separate absolute before/after impact totals. This is efficient if marginal impacts are approximately linear and stable.

For a location with reliable consumption data, vendor sales or grocery/restaurant data provide baseline volumes; where these are unavailable, national shares are multiplied by location-specific “convenience” ratings and renormalized (pp.7–9). The rationale is practical adaptability under limited data. The authors recognize that tap-water consumption is harder to observe and propose survey questions about fountain use and purchases.

Cost adjustment is motivated by loss aversion: a more expensive replacement should be penalized more strongly than an equally cheaper alternative is rewarded (p.12). A cube-root response gives diminishing sensitivity on each side of the reference price. The paper cites prospect theory, but the particular coefficient, exponent and three-to-one loss/gain response are not calibrated to drink choices. **Reviewer inference:** this is a behaviorally motivated scenario function, not an empirically established demand model.

For impact aggregation, the authors reject ordinary vector magnitude because it loses the sign of benefit or harm. They instead use signed distance to the plane `environment + economy + health = 0` (pp.17–18). The geometric reasoning correctly preserves a sign. It does not establish that the three dimensions have commensurate scales or equal social importance.

## Inputs, assumptions and acquisition gaps

National consumption is based on a USDA beverage-intake report, with bottled/tap water split using another study (pp.6–7). The chart uses a 2.55 L drinking-day total. The text refers to a survey of consumption as if it were beverage sales; these measures must remain distinct. It also repeatedly invokes “regression toward the mean” to justify regular aggregate consumption. A large sample can reduce random sampling variation, but it does not remove systematic differences in age, climate, activity, trip purpose or access.

Convenience takes subjective values between roughly 1 and 10, combining desire, availability and practical effort (pp.8–10). Airport ratings include bottled 4, tap 6, coffee 8 and dairy 1. Explanations such as travel requiring caffeine or dairy making aircraft bathrooms undesirable motivate hypotheses rather than measured effect sizes. Multiplying an ordinal rating by a volume share assumes a ratio scale: a rating of 8 has twice the effect of 4. Survey refinement is proposed, but the estimation objective, sampling plan and uncertainty are not specified.

The airport demand calculation distinguishes passengers from workers: 153,000 daily passengers at 2.22 hours and 30,000 workers at eight hours produce an approximately 3.2-hour average stay. Dividing by a 16-hour drinking day yields about 0.51 L per person, or 93,330 L/day (pp.10–11). This is a useful exposure-time adjustment. However, treating all 30,000 workers as attending daily and assuming uniform drinking throughout awake time are substantive assumptions. Passenger counts must also match the population actually exposed to the airport policy.

Concord uses 19,000 residents; its adjustments appeal to age and local wine/juice production. San Francisco uses 885,000 and applies externally reported sugary-drink-tax consumption changes to national rates (pp.13–14). The local convenience vector and transfer assumptions are not fully displayed. A large city is not automatically representative of the national average. The cited tax study's setting, population and time period need to be matched before transporting its response to this case.

The paper assumes full compliance, negligible non-PET alternatives, constant total fluids and proportional price scaling across locations (pp.5–6). Full compliance is initially defended as the intention of legislators, which does not demonstrate actual compliance; the discussion later acknowledges this weakness (p.22). Missing alternatives, beverage waste and nonproportional prices are also candidly listed.

## Model mechanics and internal audit

For baseline shares, `s_i = c_i b_i / Σ(c_j b_j)` is transparent and conserves total volume when nonnegative inputs are used. The report sometimes carries percentages into volume equations without dividing by 100 (p.9); the numerical airport table later does use the division (p.11). The post-ban denominator and the final impact sum each list tea twice and omit soda (pp.11, 18). The worked tables include soda, so these appear to be formula transcription errors, but they must be corrected before another agent implements the prose literally.

With price difference `d = PET price − alternative price`, the multiplier is `1 + [2 cbrt(d) − cbrt(|d|)]/20` (p.12). Thus cheaper alternatives receive a `cbrt(d)/20` increase while dearer ones receive a three-times-larger decrease. All remaining beverage shares are reweighted and renormalized. This changes consumption among people who were not PET buyers as well as reallocating displaced PET purchases; the predicted reduction in alcohol is a direct consequence. The authors describe increased price awareness (p.14), but do not test whether a PET ban actually creates that population-wide response. A constrained substitution-only baseline would make the additional assumption visible.

The cost multiplier needs a declared price domain and nonnegativity safeguard for general application. More fundamentally, prices are dollars per litre, while the multiplier is dimensionless; its scale coefficient implicitly has corresponding units and changes under currency conversion unless transformed consistently. Using equal proportional price markups to justify ignoring location prices is also not generally valid for a nonlinear response to absolute price differences.

Impact coefficients are assigned on approximate −1 to 1 scales with cited explanations (pp.15–17). Environmental coefficients mix resource intensity, annual emissions and national aggregate consumption. Economic coefficients are motivated by total industry revenues, although the model needs an impact per incremental litre. A larger industry need not create more marginal value per litre. Revenue is not net welfare: production costs, consumer spending, lost surplus and transfers are absent. Health coefficients describe broad beverage categories without exposure-response calibration or distinctions between sweetened and unsweetened drinks. These scores are judgments informed by sources, not measured physical or clinical impacts.

Tap water is assigned `[0, 0, 0]`; all other categories have negative summed coefficients. Consequently, shifting consumption toward tap water improves the overall score largely by construction. Fountain installation, filtering, cooling and reusable containers have costs/impacts, yet the recommended interventions are not charged to the model. Existing infrastructure does not imply zero marginal or capacity-expansion cost.

The signed projection simplifies to `(E + C + H)/√3`. It is an equal-weight additive score multiplied by a constant. Dividing by √3 changes neither rankings nor signs; a three-dimensional diagram does not provide independent justification for the value tradeoffs. The underlying vector should remain visible and the weights/scales should be varied.

The airport component totals `[1844, 2354.9, −1247]` and score about 1704 are internally consistent with the displayed impact table (pp.18–19). This is arithmetic consistency, not external validity. The final table reports 47 per 1,000 airport people (p.21), although 1704 divided by 183,000 daily people and multiplied by 1,000 is about 9.31. Approximately 47 can be obtained using 36,600 equivalent full drinking-day people, an exposure-normalized denominator. That alternative must be labeled explicitly before comparing with city residents.

## Validation, writing and supportable conclusions

There is no held-out consumption test, empirical before/after comparison, parameter sensitivity analysis or executable code. The report repeatedly uses intuitive plausibility and agreement with legislative intentions as confirmation (pp.11, 13, 19). Neither tests prediction accuracy. Numerical results support only a conditional conclusion: under the stated convenience, price-response and impact-score assumptions, the modeled substitution produces a positive composite score and a negative health component.

The recommendations—more accessible cold/filtered fountains, sugar-related taxes and packaging-related taxes—identify plausible intervention variables (p.20), but no revised numerical scenario shows their costs, feasible scale or incremental benefit. The public article's “conclusive” language overstates the evidence. Its accessible prose and before/after chart are useful communication devices, provided model scenarios are labeled as such.

The paper's strongest writing feature is modular progression: objectives precede formulas, intermediate units/tables connect stages, and limitations name missing behaviors. Its weakest inference is treating sourced narrative explanations as enough to make subjective coefficients objective. A good workflow should preserve the former and directly audit the latter.

## Transfer to the six-role workflow

The **coordinator** should create a demand-to-output map and enforce policy scope before case runs. The **domain-data** role should separate observed local volumes from national proxies and subjective convenience ratings. The **modeler** can adopt normalized share interfaces, while distinguishing displaced-buyer substitution from reoptimization of the entire population. The **computation** role should enforce nonnegative shares, conservation, percent conversion, component sums and explicit exposure denominators. The **independent-reviewer** should reduce geometric or named-method constructions to their underlying assumptions and test score/behavior sensitivity. The **writer-visuals** role should retain the staged tables and intuitive signed-impact explanation while replacing claims of confirmation with conditional findings and uncertainty.
