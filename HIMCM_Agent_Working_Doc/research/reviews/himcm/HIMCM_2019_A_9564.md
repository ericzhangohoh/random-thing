## Problem demands and full architecture

The official problem asks how public charging demand has changed and may change, resulting costs and who pays, differences among venues, initiatives and their effects on the model, plus a school-newspaper article. The paper supplies historical/forecast scenario parameters pp.4–5, separate EV and personal-device cost models pp.7–18, four venue demonstrations pp.19–20, cost incidence pp.20–21, interventions pp.21–22 and the article p.2.

The authors turn an open-ended cost question into a service-level planning problem: how many facilities are required to keep a defined dissatisfaction index below a threshold, and what capital and annual operating costs follow? There are two distinct model chains:

`EV ownership × venue traffic → stochastic arrivals and parking duration → served/blocked or waiting vehicles → need-weighted dissatisfaction + energy → station-count/cost curves.`

`Visitor flow and stay → occupied population → battery-state distribution → depletion and selective charging operators → change in unmet need; venue area/shape → access distance → impatience; combine scores → capacity/location-count choices → cost.`

For personal devices, the number of simultaneous charging slots and number of geographic charging locations are separate decisions. Model testing chooses the least fixed-plus-three-year-operating cost among combinations meeting the satisfaction target (p.19). This is a useful architecture because capacity and access are not interchangeable.

## Explicit reasons for selecting methods

The geographic scope is restricted to the United States to avoid a nominally global model with poorly transferable ownership and infrastructure assumptions (p.4). EVs and phones are split because their charging behavior, facility requirements and access constraints differ. Venues are represented by visitor/vehicle flow, stay, operating hours, area and compactness, allowing context-specific parameters without rebuilding the entire model (p.6).

EV charging is framed as a queue because vehicles compete for a limited number of parking/charging stations (p.7). In the slow-charging version, full stations cause immediate rejection; service time is parking duration because visitors came for another purpose. In emergency fast charging, low-battery drivers wait, while nonurgent drivers leave if the stations are occupied (pp.9–10). These behavioral distinctions are explicitly connected to the venue's purpose and urgency. Constant arrival intensity alone, however, does not imply a Poisson process; independence and empirical fit remain assumptions.

The personal-device model uses a steady-flow population and battery-distribution transformations to avoid simulating every phone (pp.11–14). A water-pipe/heat-loss analogy explains the balance of arrivals, departures, depletion and recharge. Spatial access is then approximated using area and shape compactness (p.15). The paper derives proportional relationships separately before combining them, a readable argument, although proportional direction alone does not establish the exact multiplicative formula or coefficient.

The intervention restricting charging to low-battery users is connected back to the model: users above 40% battery contribute no dissatisfaction under its definition, so excluding them can reduce required capacity and electricity while retaining the target score (p.22). This is a clear mechanism-to-model connection. **Reviewer inference:** its claimed benefit is partly built into the chosen welfare definition; it should also be evaluated against other fairness/service metrics.

## Data and normative assumptions

The 2019/2021 scenarios change EV share, phone ownership and charging/depletion parameters. Forecast entries are inputs, not independent validated predictions of this model (p.5). The assumed uniform ownership across venues and absence of seasonal/peak-hour variation suppress major possible heterogeneity. Grid-upgrade costs, maintenance borne through supplier contracts, tariffs varying with demand/time and standby energy are excluded (pp.5–6, 23). These define a restricted cost accounting boundary, not total social or provider life-cycle cost.

EV battery capacities are sampled from five popular models with sales-based weights; initial battery percentage is uniform. Level-2 charging is specified as 6.6 kW, installation as $3,000, and electricity as $0.11/kWh; fast charging uses 50 kW and $21,000 (pp.8–10). These are historical scenario inputs. Uniform initial battery level is not validated, and vehicle sales shares need not equal the visiting fleet distribution.

Personal-device need and charging propensity use separately specified logistic-shaped functions. Charging facilities are assumed effectively occupied 80% of the time; a 25-hour battery lifetime and 5V/2A charging produce a charging/depletion ratio of about fourteen (pp.13–14). The personal-device index uses 0.6 need and 0.4 impatience, justified qualitatively by urgency (p.16). Recommended dissatisfaction thresholds of 0.05 for EVs and 0.2 for phones are judgments without a user study. The phone need index can be negative when charging increases battery levels, despite the earlier statement that all indices lie in [0,1] (pp.6, 14).

## Validation, scenarios and supportable conclusions

EV simulations repeat each facility count 500 times and show error bars, providing evidence about simulation variability under fixed assumptions (pp.8–10, 34–37). The paper does not clearly specify whether the bars represent standard deviations, standard errors or intervals. Repetition does not validate the arrival process, battery distribution or dissatisfaction function.

The coverage approximation predicts about fifteen charging locations for Bold Park, and a manually drawn circle arrangement also uses fifteen (p.15). This is a useful geometric plausibility check, not a proof of minimum coverage or actual walkable distance. Obstacles, paths, inaccessible land and overlap are not encoded by compactness alone. More coverage tests are promised in the appendix, but the appendix instead contains code and EV simulation plots without additional mapped coverage cases.

Airport, park, school and café examples exercise substantially different scales and explain why distributed small chargers suit a large low-density park (pp.19–20). These are scenario demonstrations, not observed installations or measured costs. Using Singapore Changi and an Australian park with U.S.-based prices/ownership requires explicit interpretation as geometry/traffic scenarios, rather than local predictions. The authors acknowledge that airport traffic growth is absent. The claim that EV costs nearly double while phone costs rise little is conditional on the supplied two-year forecast and service thresholds.

## Mathematical and implementation audit

- The slow-charging appendix sets every vehicle's charging rate to 50 kW, rather than the main model's 6.6 kW (p.25 versus p.8). This materially changes energy costs while parking occupancy can remain unchanged. Its displayed runs use other venue/time parameters, with no run manifest tying output figures to code versions.
- Fast-charging dissatisfaction is described as increasing linearly to one at half an hour, but the code returns `wait` below 0.5 and one above it, creating a jump instead of the stated normalized line (p.27). The main need-index piecewise intervals also overlap from 5% to 9%; the slow-code boundary uses 9% consistently (pp.8, 25).
- Phone cost prose switches from 5V/2A when calculating charge speed to 5V/1A when calculating energy (pp.14, 16). MATLAB operating-cost expressions omit the stated 0.004 kW-per-slot factor, while result construction uses $2,000 per point after the body and search cost use $200 (pp.31–33). The airport table's $394,000 for 197 points follows $2,000, not $200 (p.19). Results require regeneration before reuse.
- The printed impatience function lacks the factor of distance in its lower branch; the appendix uses a logistic function instead of the prose's capped linear function (pp.16, 31). Distances alternate between kilometres and area in square metres without a consistently documented conversion.
- The capacity fraction is capped at one in the prose, but the battery-change appendix uses its uncapped value; the demand-propensity diagonal is also applied again in the score (pp.14, 30–31). Conservation, nonnegative implied distributions and exact score correspondence need explicit checks.
- The steady-state battery distribution is imposed as a normalized Gaussian-shaped profile. Independent identically distributed batteries do not imply a normal distribution (p.12). The balance-derived entry/exit differences should be checked for feasible distributions instead of assumed to establish them.
- The cost-incidence section equates supply/demand slopes with elasticities and uses vendor sales-uplift claims as support for reciprocity (pp.20–21). These do not establish causal revenue offsets. Fees reduce a provider's net burden, not necessarily physical electricity consumption.

## Transfer to the working document

Reuse the separation of access from capacity, explicit service standards, context-specific queue behavior, repeated stochastic trials, and intervention-to-parameter mapping. Retain the accessible article's practical user recommendations, while linking any headline cost number to one verified run.

A demand agent should own scenario provenance and uncertainty; a service-model agent should define who counts in the dissatisfaction denominator; a spatial agent should distinguish coverage from network walking access; a cost agent should maintain units and cost scope; and an independent validator should reconcile equations, implementation and every reported table. The integrator should reject an intervention that appears beneficial solely because the metric excludes affected users without clearly stating that value judgment. These role requirements are **reviewer inference**, not instructions from the original authors.
