## Demand map and scope fidelity

The official task requires positive and negative impacts of a precisely defined sales ban, required data and collection methods, application to Concord and San Francisco, airport adaptation, revised policies represented in the model, generalization to larger communities, and a one-page local-newspaper article. The paper supplies a competition model on pp.7–11, impact equations and data-source suggestions on pp.12–18, three applications on pp.19–22, recommendations in those applications, and the article on p.4. It does not actually rerun an explicitly revised policy or develop a systematic larger-region generalization analysis.

Scope errors arise before the modeling. The summary calls the ban one on PET bottles under 500 ml; later categories use less than 1 L versus at least 1 L (pp.1, 10). The official Concord threshold is **1 L or less**, plain nonsparkling water in PET, and the restriction concerns **sales**, not possession or use. The FAQ expressly permits free distribution. The paper repeatedly describes a use ban and treats the disappearance of PET pollution much more broadly than the policy permits (pp.5, 12, 14). San Francisco's restriction on city property is also not separated cleanly from a citywide market removal. These differences change the counterfactual and substitution set; they are not minor wording issues.

## Model architecture

The main idea is `policy shock → beverage market shares over time → changes in material volumes → environmental, fiscal, employment, facility and personal impacts`. The shared market module is intended to prevent each impact branch from inventing its own substitution behavior. This is the paper's most useful architectural contribution.

The authors start with two-species competitive Lotka–Volterra equations, introduce a minimum share through an additional `(x_i − L_i)` factor, and represent a third category as the residual share. A five-category extension distinguishes small PET water, large PET water, other beverages, reusable glass water and tap water. Four differential equations determine the first four shares; the fifth is the residual required to sum to one (pp.7–11). A ban changes the small-bottle growth parameter to a negative value, creating a transition between pre-ban and post-ban steady scenarios.

The impact branches use linear accounting relationships: unrecycled plastic mass; energy/associated emissions after recycling credit; tax revenues; workers implied by production; waste-treatment expenditure; fountain installation; consumer spending; a sugar-exposure index; and emergency supplies (pp.12–16). Numerical applications emphasize six outputs: plastic waste, energy, tax, waste treatment, fountains and sugar. Employment, individual expenditure and emergency costs are explicitly left unevaluated for lack of data (p.18).

## Author rationale versus methodological inference

The authors explicitly justify Lotka–Volterra by comparing beverage firms competing for a limited customer market with species competing for resources (p.7). Lower bounds are introduced because an ordinary beverage category should not disappear immediately. Starting with two, then three, then five categories is an explicit effort to develop complexity gradually and avoid estimating too many coefficients (pp.8–10).

**Reviewer inference:** substitution is indeed the central mechanism, but ecological analogy alone does not identify market dynamics or causal policy response. Cross-price choice, consumer preferences, retail availability and geography are not represented. The statement that goods generally grow linearly is unsupported, and removing one growth factor does not make the resulting coupled system linear. Pairwise interaction coefficients grow quadratically with category count; the paper's claim that computational complexity necessarily grows exponentially is not established.

The model needs simplex invariance, not only a sum-to-one identity. Defining tap water as `1 − sum(other shares)` can generate a negative residual unless parameter constraints or numerical projection prevent it. Lower bounds also conflict with the later statement that a banned category reaches exactly zero: with a positive lower equilibrium, a negative growth parameter alone does not document a valid removal rule. No solver, step size, boundary treatment, equilibrium tolerance or complete coefficient table is given (pp.8–11). These are essential implementation specifications for transferring the approach.

## Data and scenario assumptions

The problem explicitly permits proposed data collection rather than actual collection. The paper names sensible broad sources: government waste/recycling records, production interviews, tax-administration data, scientific energy reports and consumer surveys for market interactions (pp.9, 12–14). However, a usable collection plan would need sample frames, time periods, pre/post observations, beverage units and a way to estimate interaction parameters. These are mostly absent.

Concord is assigned a population of 130,000 and described in relation to New Hampshire, whereas the supplied problem identifies Concord, Massachusetts with approximately 19,000 residents (pp.16–17). A later example assumes total demand 12,000 and initial shares 6:2:2 without reconciling that scale to the earlier 195,000 litres/month estimate (p.19). The Concord and San Francisco impact tables have identical baseline totals despite their different populations and assumed per-person demand (pp.19–20).

City recycling is set to 95%, airport recycling to 100%, based on bin availability and cleaners. Correct collection is not identical to successful material recycling. Fiscal-adjustment factors of 1, 0.5 and 0.1; profit factors of 0.5 and 1; and sugar-preference factors of 1, 0.1 and 0.5 are assigned through qualitative stories (pp.16–18). These are scenario assumptions, not measured differences or calibrated estimates. The assumption that hydration need is inelastic does not establish that demand for one packaging category is perfectly price-inelastic (p.6).

## Validation and internal consistency

There is no empirical calibration/validation, withheld time series, uncertainty interval or systematic sensitivity study. The changing competition-coefficient plots on p.8 illustrate model behavior; they do not validate beverage-market behavior. The explicitly assumed scenarios can illustrate potential substitutions and conflicting impacts. They cannot support the claim that the ban has been proved beneficial or predict actual municipal outcomes.

Several material inconsistencies require resolution:

- Concord energy rises from 13,849.0 to 130,004.9 kJ in its table, while the nearby prose says energy falls and the percentage chart shows a small decrease. This is a substantive result disagreement, not a rounding issue (pp.19–20).
- Airport recycling is asserted to eliminate plastic pollution, but its numerical table still reports plastic waste falling from 3.96 to 1.88 tonnes (pp.17, 21–22).
- The five-category model uses litres/market volumes, yet the tax and employment notation changes meanings; employment divides litres by bottles produced per worker without a bottle-volume conversion (p.13).
- The sugar expression subtracts a daily per-person allowance from grams per litre before applying volume/share factors. It does not have a coherent exposure unit as written and cannot be interpreted as a disease-rate model (pp.15, 17).
- Fountain demand uses `x3`, which denotes packaged beverages in the five-category model rather than tap water. Whether the bracket operator rounds up, down or to nearest is unspecified (pp.10, 14).
- Consumer incremental cost multiplies manufacturing-cost change by a profit rate rather than defining price, markup and pass-through; emergency production-line claims assume a local sales ban removes external production availability (pp.15–16).

The displayed carbon-footprint formulas were visually checked: their conversion factor is in the denominator. Text extraction makes this look like multiplication in one location, demonstrating why mathematical review must use the rendered source when extraction is ambiguous (pp.12, 17).

## Recommendations, argument and workflow transfer

Recommendations include taxing sweet drinks and fountain use, improving fountain appearance, informing consumers and optimizing fountain placement (pp.20–22). They respond to the chosen impact branches, but are not tied to estimated parameter changes or a solved revised scenario. Price-response modeling is especially necessary before claiming that taxation changes the substitution pattern. A fountain-location proposal requires spatial accessibility constraints rather than total water capacity alone.

The article is visually clear and acknowledges uncertainty from assumed data. The technical report has a readable progression from one central mechanism to several stakeholder outcomes and recognizes that lower plastic waste can coexist with higher sugar intake or fiscal burdens. Those are strong argument-design lessons. The repeated causal language and contradictory numerical outputs, however, overstate what the scenarios establish.

For the working document, assign a **problem interpreter** to turn the supplied policy into a precise rule table before modeling; a **behavioral modeler** to estimate or bound substitutions; a **data planner** to specify observations capable of identifying those responses; and a **validator** to check conservation, nonnegative shares, units and table/plot agreement. Require every recommendation to produce a revised parameter, constraint or mechanism and a comparable output. The generalizable approach is shared substitution accounting across multiple impacts; the reported municipal effects should not be reused as established evidence.
