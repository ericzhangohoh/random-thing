## Demand map and model architecture

The official problem asks for a specification-based ranking, top ten, comparison with two online systems, an app concept and one-page news release. The paper addresses the substantive components but begins with a two-page release followed by the abstract (pp.1–3), rather than the requested summary-first, one-page release arrangement. Historical paper examples are therefore not templates for submission compliance.

The analysis pipeline is:

`Specification audit → physical/statistical imputation and categorical repair → excitement/safety/innovation hierarchy → triangular fuzzy pairwise judgments → logarithmic fuzzy preference optimization → entropy weights → blended component scores → ranking → rank-distance sensitivity and external comparison → revised clipping policy → personalized app.`

Eleven original factors expand to sixteen criterion-specific entries because speed, type, year and other fields can play different roles in different categories (pp.15–18). The category weights are approximately 64.6% excitement, 22.8% safety and 12.6% innovation (p.18). The app applies user preferences to these weights and adds discovery, ticketing, mapping and feedback functions (pp.24–32).

## Method choices and their stated justification

The authors first recognize a semantic issue in the data: the speed field is a maximum, so length divided by this speed is not ride duration (p.7). They use an ideal energy relation for speed/drop and a curvature relation plus regression for G-force, then use SPSS imputation where they cannot justify a direct mechanistic equation (pp.7–9). This division between mechanisms and empirical estimation is useful, provided each method's assumptions are checked.

A triangular fuzzy number expresses uncertainty in pairwise importance judgments rather than a single exact AHP ratio (pp.10–11). Taking logarithms turns ratios into differences. Slack variables penalize inconsistencies and a nonlinear program yields normalized positive weights (pp.12–13). The mathematical transformation has an explicit computational purpose, rather than appearing as unexplained sophistication.

Entropy weights describe variation in specification data; fuzzy weights represent survey preferences. The authors explicitly blend them and give the preference model greater influence because the entropy results differ from expectations and categorical encodings behave poorly (pp.14–15). Code later makes the blend 20% entropy and 80% fuzzy weight for applicable components (pp.38–39). They exclude arbitrary categorical encodings from entropy calculations rather than pretending that codes 1, 2 and 3 have ratio-scale meaning.

**Reviewer inference:** acknowledging different information sources is a strength, but the blend is a modeling choice requiring justification and sensitivity analysis. Greater agreement with expectations does not establish a better objective specification-only ranking. Survey-driven weights are more naturally justified for the personalized app extension.

## Data, assumptions and interfaces

Missingness counts are reported before imputation, including 218 missing G-force and 209 missing angle values (p.7). The physical completion assumes negligible drag/friction and gravity-driven motion; launched rides and varying track geometry challenge that applicability. The radius regression `r = 2.4652h − 189.01` needs explicit units and a positive-radius domain before it can infer G-force (p.8). The spreadsheet contains feet and mph while the notation table uses SI units; a documented conversion interface is essential.

SPSS is named, but the prose describes EM in nearest-neighbor terms and does not give a reproducible distributional model, variable selection or convergence configuration (p.9). Corrected categorical records and a sample completed table are shown (pp.9–10). The initial assumption that all data are correct is therefore inconsistent with the later repair work (p.6).

The survey is described as hundreds of respondents without a sample frame, exact count, question-level results or uncertainty (p.16). Fuzzy matrices and output weights are visible (pp.17–19), which helps audit the calculations, but does not recover the underlying observations. “Safety” is represented through physical specifications/material/year preferences, without accident or reliability data. It is a perceived/design-based score, not a measured safety risk.

## Validation and the revision narrative

Weight sensitivity changes individual weights by 20% and 50% and measures ranking changes using a Kendall-distance idea (pp.15, 21). This is a better connection to the actual ranking output than examining only an aggregate score. Length and vertical angle show relatively large responses in the displayed charts. The measure is confusingly called the time needed to make a ranking, although the intended quantity is rearrangement count, not computational runtime.

External comparison uses an intersection of 43 coasters from two lists and compares high/low groups (pp.22–23). The paper investigates why clipping standardized values to [−1,1] suppressed exceptional specifications, removes the caps and shows a revised ranking (pp.23–24). This is an instructive model-debugging episode: normalization is part of the model and can alter the decision.

However, the external lists were used both to motivate the adjustment and to support the claim that the adjusted ranking is accurate. Improved agreement is therefore a calibration result, not independent validation. Geographic exposure and different ranking populations are plausible explanations for disagreement, not tested causes. There is no displayed imputation holdout assessment, blend-weight sensitivity or validation of the safety construct.

## Static consistency and reproduction issues

The initial world top-ten table has GateKeeper first (p.19), whereas the geographic discussion says the first coaster is in Abu Dhabi (p.22). The comparison tables also use the restricted 43-item universe, so the report needs separate identifiers for full-universe, intersection and revised rankings rather than treating all lists as one result.

The appendix still clips the input values at ±1 even though the final narrative removes that clipping (p.38). Its sensitivity loop repeatedly modifies the same weight vector and normalizes it without restoring the baseline between factors (p.39). As written, later columns are cumulative perturbations, not independent one-factor changes. The prose describes redistributing the increase equally, while the code uses proportional normalization. These are static inconsistencies requiring a rerun from a fixed baseline.

The fuzzy solver imposes 0–2 bounds on all decision variables, including logged weights, membership and slack variables (pp.35–38). Those finite bounds need justification against the derivation; an optimizer returning a result does not establish that it solves the intended unrestricted formulation. External workbooks are required, so the printed code alone is not a complete reproduction package.

## Transfer to the working document

Require the researcher to define each field's meaning before using a physical equation. Require a construct map separating excitement, perceived safety and observed reliability. Retain the explicit explanation of why uncertain judgments motivate fuzzy intervals and why logarithms simplify the optimization. Have the validator distinguish tuning from held-out evaluation and reset a stored baseline for every sensitivity run. Have the integrator freeze scoring transforms and data-universe identifiers before the writer produces comparisons. For app concepts, keep the user journey concrete and mark social-data access, recommendation intelligence and launch claims as proposed capabilities rather than demonstrated operations.
