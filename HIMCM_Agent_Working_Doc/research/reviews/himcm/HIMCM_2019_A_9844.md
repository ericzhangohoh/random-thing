## Demand map and architecture

The official problem requires historical and future public charging consumption, costs and who pays, venue differences, initiatives with modeled effects, and a school-newspaper article. The paper addresses consumption on pp.5–10, cost structure on pp.11–13, café/airport/mall/school examples on pp.14–18, initiatives on pp.18–21, and the article on p.22. The eight-panel sensitivity appendix is p.23; code occupies pp.23–28.

Its architecture is `ownership histories → fitted adoption relationships and battery trends → public charging energy → infrastructure/operation/user-time cost components → venue payment rules → intervention multipliers`. EVs and smartphones receive separate demand and cost equations. Their outputs are joined for venues serving both. This separation helps expose which assumptions transfer and which require venue-specific evidence.

There is no implemented queueing or capacity-allocation model. Infrastructure is assumed to match demand, waiting is effectively removed in the numerical scenarios, and the expression “min” attached to the cost function is not accompanied by a specified feasible optimization problem. The main numerical method is scenario accounting.

## Selection rationale: stated versus inferred

The authors select a Bass-style adoption model because innovation and imitation can explain product uptake, and describe it as simple and computationally economical (p.8). Those are explicit rationales. They do not empirically compare forecast accuracy or runtime against alternative methods. **Reviewer inference:** adoption theory is a sensible candidate family for ownership data, but the actual fitted equations must retain that family's defining constraints before its theoretical interpretation can justify them.

The paper explicitly distinguishes electricity charged in public from total device consumption, and gross-ups delivered energy for charging losses (pp.5–7). This is a useful modeling improvement over assigning every user's daily energy requirement to public venues. It also decomposes equipment, land, electricity, maintenance and user waiting costs rather than reporting an unexplained lump sum (pp.11–13).

Venue cases are chosen to represent different commercial and institutional settings, with proposed recovery through coffee sales, parking, air tickets, rent or institutional funding (pp.14–18). These are proposed payment mechanisms; the analysis does not establish how customers would respond to the price changes. Passing a charge to another stakeholder changes its incidence, not automatically its physical resource cost.

## Inputs, assumptions and model interfaces

The study uses eight annual ownership observations, 2011–2018. EV demand combines stock, a pure-electric share, 50 km/day, 18 kWh/100 km, a 7% public charging fraction and charging loss. Smartphone demand combines stock, an average capacity from two recent iPhone models, 3.8V, 0.68 charges/day, a 51% public fraction and 39% loss (pp.5–7). US data availability motivates the geographic choice; plug-in hybrids and laptops are excluded (p.4).

The iPhone proxy's representativeness for all smartphones is assumed rather than demonstrated. Multiplying EV stock by a market share also requires stock and share to describe compatible populations, not a stock and a year's sales share. Some citations do not support their attached claims: reference [2] is a phone-battery article but is attached to EV inputs, and the initial citation near Bass selection points to a portable-charger patent, although a Bass reference appears elsewhere (pp.7–8, 21).

Interfaces need explicit units and accounting periods. Land and installation are one-time capital costs, while electricity and operations are annual flows; the examples add them without a consistent annualization rule or clear first-year label. Some formulas switch between dollars, ten-thousand-dollar units and yuan labels (pp.11–13). Waiting time is alternately described as total waiting and multiplied by a vehicle count; a cost sum first includes waiting time before a monetary conversion is expanded (p.12). These ambiguities prevent the aggregate cost from being interpreted confidently.

## Forecast construction and validation

The stated Bass recurrence has an increment containing a **negative** quadratic coefficient, `−q/m`, when innovation/imitation parameters and market capacity have their usual positive domains (p.8). The EV fitted equation instead has a positive quadratic coefficient. Moreover, MATLAB fits next-year ownership against previous-year ownership, while the text labels the fitted response as the ownership increment (pp.8–9, 24). Omitting the subtraction of previous ownership changes the linear term. The code does not recover and constrain the claimed Bass parameters. The label therefore supplies more theoretical assurance than the fitted implementation supports.

Forecast arrays for EVs and smartphones are entered directly in the appendix rather than generated by a displayed iterative forecast routine (p.25). The main demand model uses an EV loss rate of 0.10, whereas the code uses 0.16. The battery-capacity extrapolation describes an annual increment of 250 mAh, but the last listed forecast rises by 300 mAh; its averaging formula also divides eight observations' successive changes by eight rather than the seven available intervals (pp.9–10).

The fit diagrams are in-sample. There is no withheld-year forecast evaluation or interval propagation. A comparison between a 3.2-million forecast and an external 3.3-million forecast is a plausibility comparison between predictions, not observed validation, and matching vehicle scope is not established (p.10). The supportable result is that the chosen assumptions produce increasing projected public charging energy. The exact projections and their claimed diffusion interpretation remain unverified.

## Cost examples and sensitivity: useful structure, unreliable totals

Several calculations illustrate why a readable ledger still needs independent arithmetic review:

- The café case derives 30 sockets but its capital expression uses only 20 and omits part of its stated wiring cost. Its maintenance expression also evaluates `(12 + 20) × 30` inconsistently (pp.14–15).
- Airport phone costs of 150,400 and 1,342,329.4 are summed as 31,492,729.4 in the text, while the appendix computes the smaller arithmetic sum. The resulting per-visitor charge inherits the text error (pp.15–16, 26).
- Mall EV electricity uses a 5W quantity in a context otherwise using kilowatt EV charging. The reported phone-energy product and per-customer charge are inconsistent with their displayed component arithmetic (pp.16–17).
- The school example transfers a stated tariff across locations without documenting comparability. One charger per estimated EV does not establish continuous full utilization for all modeled hours (p.17).
- The grouped chart's phone/vehicle labels are reversed relative to the ordering of its arrays. An airport operation constant in the sensitivity code differs by roughly an order of magnitude from the baseline calculation (pp.18, 26–27).

These are implementation findings, not numerical reproduction. They are sufficiently material that the published venue totals and recommended relative savings should not be reused as trusted estimates.

Eight initiatives act on land, construction, operation, phone equipment or recovered-payment multipliers (pp.18–21). Parameter sweeps show which assumed component changes matter under the cost formula. They do not estimate achievable effects. For example, solar panels are associated with land/capital reductions in parts of the implementation, although the prose discusses electricity savings and added capital. Free parking, upselling and ticket surcharges also require forgone-revenue or demand-response terms before becoming net savings. The sensitivity plots on p.23 largely reflect linear component magnitudes and inherited baseline errors, rather than independent evidence of intervention effectiveness.

## Argument, writing and workflow transfer

The paper's strongest reusable feature is the progression from demand drivers to named cost components to specific payment units. The newspaper article demonstrates an audience change: it translates the modeling question into recognizable school and public-space decisions (p.22). Its numerical claims still require the same verification as the technical report.

For an agent workflow, this paper motivates four concrete controls. A **model architect** must check that a chosen method has the mathematical properties invoked to justify it; a Bass label cannot repair an unconstrained polynomial. A **data steward** must verify denominator compatibility, public-use fractions, geography and claim-level citations. An **implementation reviewer** must reconcile every published number, chart label, constant and forecast array with one frozen calculation. A **decision analyst** must distinguish physical cost reduction, capital timing and redistribution of payment.

The generalizable justification chain is promising: “these observable drivers create this demand; these cost components follow from that demand; this initiative changes this component.” The paper also shows where that chain breaks: unsupported proxies, a theory/code mismatch, mixed accounting periods and interventions represented by arbitrary discounts. Use the architecture as a design example and the discrepancies as mandatory audit cases; do not adopt its numerical conclusions as established evidence.
