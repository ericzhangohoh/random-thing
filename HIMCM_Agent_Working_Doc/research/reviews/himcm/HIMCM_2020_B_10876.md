## Problem interpretation and model chain

The authors treat the task as scheduling conservation projects under a maximum annual fundraising capacity. A project must be funded continuously once started. Unspent annual capacity cannot be saved, project costs remain fixed when shifted, and success probability declines with delay. These assumptions create a resource-constrained scheduling problem: choose start years, respect annual expenditure limits, and maximize the sum of project-level expected-benefit/cost ratios.

The paper proceeds from characteristics and board objectives to an objective function, a delay model, a random-permutation scheduling algorithm, and several budget scenarios. The sequence is easy to follow, and the four-project block example makes the algorithm understandable without requiring code literacy. The distinction between a priority order and an actual calendar is explicit and useful.

The official prompt asks for long-term reliable funding for all 48 projects and future management. The no-savings assumption removes the central endowment mechanism rather than modeling it. Reporting annual expenses as the required annual donations addresses a narrower operating-budget question. No endowment balance, investment return, reserve, uncertain donations, inflation, or terminal management liability appears. The fixed-budget solution also explicitly drops one project, so it cannot be presented as satisfying the all-48 requirement.

## Approach and justification

For project i, the objective contribution is benefit multiplied by delayed success probability, divided by total project cost. The total objective is the **sum of these ratios**, not total expected conservation benefit divided by total spending. That distinction matters: a cheap project receives a larger scheduling weight even when a costly project creates more absolute expected benefit. Since all included projects eventually receive their fixed cost stream, shifting start dates does not lower their undiscounted total cost. The objective therefore primarily chooses whose expected benefit is lost to delay; it does not independently accomplish the claimed objective of reducing total fundraising.

The paper discusses taxonomic uniqueness, then prioritizes the two most unique projects in the code. Uniqueness is absent from the displayed objective and formal constraints. The implemented policy is consequently lexicographic in part: two projects are forced first, with random search over the remainder. This could be a defensible board decision, but it should be stated in the mathematical formulation and tested against a policy without the forced precedence.

The official data describe benefit as an expected relative conservation value that already considers threat and ease of recovery. Multiplying it by success probability requires a careful semantic check against that definition; the paper simply treats benefit as a conditional reward upon success. That interpretation risks counting recovery feasibility twice.

The delay model combines a logarithmic relationship between population size and persistence with exponential population decline, producing a common linear reduction in success probability of approximately 0.0112 per delayed year. The authors cite a simulated example, rescale its population counts to a general population-size heuristic, then borrow a decline rate from another species and location. This is a transparent attempt to justify an assumed delay penalty, but it does not validate a common coefficient for 48 unrelated recovery projects. Persistence without intervention and conditional success of a fully funded intervention are also different quantities. A scenario range for the penalty is more defensible than treating this chain as a calibrated prediction.

The logarithmic expression is not inherently bounded by zero and one. A stated range beside the equation does not implement clipping or establish that every scheduled start remains inside that range. Probability-domain checks are required at the actual selected start years.

## Algorithm, feasibility, and evidence

A random project order is converted to a schedule by placing each project as early as the remaining annual capacity permits. Later projects cannot jump ahead of a blocked earlier project. The four-project illustration explains this no-overtaking convention clearly. It also shows unused capacity; that capacity cannot be carried forward under the authors' assumption.

The current-year test in the flowchart and code checks a new project's first-year expenditure against available funds. This alone is sufficient for all future years only under additional conditions, such as nonincreasing individual cost profiles and nondecreasing capacity. The exploratory graph suggests costs generally decline, but a general trend is not a row-by-row proof of the invariant. A robust scheduler should reserve every future commitment and verify the full calendar after every insertion.

The mathematical expenditure identity sums over calendar years 1 through project duration while using a shifted project-year index. For a delayed project this omits later project years. The identity should instead sum unshifted project years, or sum calendar years through the actual completion date. The formulation also needs explicit integer start-year domains and zero cost after the project finishes.

The best-so-far curve plateaus after roughly 2,200 random permutations out of the approximately 5,000 displayed. This demonstrates lack of further improvement in that run, not closeness to the global optimum. There is no bound, exact small-instance comparison, repeated-seed distribution, or comparison against a greedy/local-search baseline. Exhaustive enumeration and uniform random permutations are not the only possible approaches. A baseline followed by swap/insertion search would make additional computation interpretable.

The schedule heatmaps and annual budget curves are useful complementary outputs: one identifies project timing; the other shows the aggregate cash requirement. However, heatmaps cannot substitute for a machine-readable start-year table and expenditure ledger. The increasing-budget annual expenditure plot ends at year 30 while remaining expenditure is visibly positive, so it does not by itself certify full-horizon funding.

## Reported findings and internal consistency

The authors report an approximately $1.05 million fixed annual capacity, a 31-year arrangement, first-year expenditure of $1,034,015.58, and exclusion of an unaffordable project. They also report that a linearly growing budget makes that project possible in year 10, and recommend approximately $1.71 million as a favorable fixed capacity. These are author results, not independently verified schedules or recommendations.

Several cross-artifact discrepancies deserve an explicit review check:

- The summary identifies the excluded project as 551, whereas the body and image-only memo identify 415. The fixed-budget heatmap shows 551 funded and 415 blank, supporting the body/memo interpretation.
- The summary's initial-project list ends with 532; the memo ends with 502. A canonical exported schedule should generate both lists automatically.
- The text says “no more than half” of $1,316,040 is available, but gives $1,052,800, approximately 80%, not half. The code uses 80% of $1,316,000. This changes the feasible set materially.
- A “1.3 times” recommendation is relative to total stated income of approximately $1.316 million, not to the $1.053 million initial project allocation. Those denominators should not be interchanged.
- The named funding reference is the Fish & Wildlife Foundation of Florida annual report. The paper treats its giving figure as FRPCE fundraising capacity without a demonstrated allocation bridge.

The $1.71 million recommendation is selected visually around a bend in the cost-effectiveness curve. It is a possible negotiation point, not a mathematical optimum: no marginal fundraising cost, board willingness to pay, risk tolerance, or explicit knee-selection rule is supplied.

## Sensitivity and code audit

Sensitivity plots vary the common decline rate, annual capacity, and annual growth of capacity. They are useful scenario explorations, but the random search is rerun without reporting seed controls or uncertainty bands. Jaggedness therefore mixes search variability with model sensitivity. Showing a broadly plausible curve does not establish decision stability; report how start years, excluded projects, minimum cash balances, and objective regret change.

There is also a structural result available before simulation: with a common linear delay coefficient, fixed included projects, and no probability truncation, the objective equals a constant minus that coefficient times a weighted sum of delays. Changing the magnitude of the common positive penalty does **not** change the exact maximizing schedule. The paper's suggestion that a more negative common growth rate inherently changes which high-benefit projects are prioritized requires clipping, species-specific rates, changed constraints, or another mechanism. Otherwise observed schedule changes can arise from heuristic sampling. This is an algebraic observation, not numerical reproduction.

The appendix is a sensitivity-search excerpt rather than a complete reproducibility package. Static inspection identifies:

- Data loading, imports, workbook schema, and a random seed are not supplied.
- Rows 22 and 37 are forcibly placed first, so the code does not sample all 48! permutations described in the exposition.
- The probability guard adds the delay penalty when checking positivity, while the score subtracts it. A delayed negative success probability can therefore pass the guard.
- The coefficient loop uses 20% through 190%, but the plotted x-values are constructed from 50% through 220%. The displayed x-axis and evaluated budgets are misaligned in this excerpt.
- Only the best scalar score is retained. The corresponding arrangement is not copied when the score improves, so this code alone does not export the best timetable.
- There is no explicit post-schedule audit of all future expenditures or a documented rule for cost lookups beyond the original data horizon. Constant-capacity infeasibility also needs a termination condition before entering a year-advancing loop.

## Transferable workflow lessons

1. Translate “all projects” and “future management” into nonnegotiable coverage and terminal-funding constraints before selecting an optimization objective.
2. Give every financial number an entity, period, currency, allocation fraction, and denominator. Annual organizational donations are not automatically project-specific capacity.
3. Explain whether the objective is total benefit, sum of ratios, or a portfolio ratio. Prove how it represents the board's decision.
4. Turn informal feasibility arguments into executable invariants. Reserve full project commitments, reconcile every source cost year, and export an audited cash ledger.
5. Use a small worked example to communicate an algorithm, then use an exactly solved small instance and multiple random seeds to evaluate its quality.
6. Perform algebraic sensitivity analysis before running parameter sweeps; some apparent model parameters scale the objective without changing its optimizer.
7. Generate the summary, memo, tables, and charts from the same accepted result artifact. Check excluded IDs, first-year starts, funding fractions, and plotted parameter values together.

The paper is strongest as a clear introduction to converting priority orders into constrained schedules and communicating multiple funding scenarios. Its objective semantics, endowment mismatch, feasibility checks, and cross-artifact consistency require repair before adopting its recommendations.
