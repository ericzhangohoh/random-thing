## Demand coverage and complete architecture

The official 2018 A task calls for specification-based ranking, a top ten, comparison with two existing lists, a user-facing app concept and a news release. The paper addresses these through two related but distinct systems:

`Provided specifications → normalization/outlier handling → mean or neural-network imputation → indicator selection and grey relation analysis → entropy-weighted scoring → ranking/sensitivity/external comparisons.`

`Expanded descriptive/preferences data → fuzzy evaluation and user filters → item-based and tag-based recommendation engines → combined ranking → UI, user scenario and proposed evaluation.`

The report follows these stages visibly, and explicitly distinguishes an objective specification ranking from recommendations that include personal circumstances (pp.6, 23–25). The physical p.3 is a promotional poster, not missing technical text. The substantial app design is within the prompt's conceptual scope; it is not evidence that an implemented app or user dataset exists.

## Why these methods were selected

The authors choose different missing-data methods according to prevalence: mean substitution for a few missing values and a backpropagation neural network for heavily missing fields (pp.8–12). Their stated reason for the latter is learning relationships between observed and missing attributes, but the claimed comparison with other methods has no displayed benchmark (p.9). The neural network section derives propagation and training rather than merely naming the method.

The indicator-selection stage removes unavailable/unquantifiable fields and attempts to prevent redundant information with grey relation analysis (pp.12–15). Binary inversion status and count are explicitly recognized as redundant (p.13). Entropy weighting is chosen over AHP because expert judgments would conflict with the desired objectivity (p.16). PCA is introduced to reduce dimension while retaining 90.576% of variance in four components (p.17), though its actual interface with the scoring calculation is unclear.

For the app, ItemCF is justified by the relatively stable and much smaller item universe compared with users, and by the desire to reuse learned item similarities (pp.28–29). Tag-based recommendations supply another relationship between preferences and item descriptions; popularity penalties and tag-cleaning address concentration and noise (pp.30–32). The design discusses precision, recall, coverage and diversity rather than equating recommender quality with accuracy alone (pp.27–28).

**Reviewer inference:** these are useful examples of writing method-selection reasons in terms of the task's data structure. They do not establish that every added method improves the end result. In particular, a complexity budget should require an explicit input/output contribution from both grey screening and PCA.

## Data and assumptions

The analysis assumes authoritative website values are reliable, excludes design defects and treats physical actions as within riders' tolerance (p.7). A higher year, height, speed and length are treated as beneficial. The type multipliers 1.00, 1.15, 1.10, 1.10 and 1.20 encode unvalidated differences between ride types (p.13). Their presence means the system includes judgments even though entropy weights are data-derived.

The outlier process deletes values outside three standard deviations and imputes them; Smiler's 14 inversions is the stated example (p.8). An extreme specification is precisely what may distinguish a leading coaster. An outlier flag therefore needs external verification before replacement; rarity is not evidence of an error. Imputation can also erase the signal needed by the ranking.

The app additionally needs behavior counts, behavior weights, interaction logs, tags and contextual attributes (pp.24–32). These are proposed future inputs, not supplied in the competition spreadsheet. The report acknowledges a new-user buffer period late in the app discussion (p.34), qualifying earlier optimistic cold-start claims (p.29).

## Validation and limits

The paper presents neural-network error curves and a prediction/observation plot for drop values (p.11). The displayed error near 0.0348 is described as 3.48%, but the appendix accumulates half-squared error over training samples (pp.42–43), not an identified percentage metric. Sample counts, split identifiers and a clearly separated validation protocol are absent. A visually close fit cannot establish comparable accuracy for every imputed field or missingness mechanism.

Rank sensitivity is more specific: increase/decrease an indicator until ordering changes (pp.18–19). Height has a narrow reported interval of −4% to +2%, while year and length have wider intervals. That indicates sensitivity to height; the broad claim of a stable ranking needs to retain this qualification. Comparing whole score-curve shapes does not establish stability of adjacent top positions.

Two external lists are compared through rank overlap, mean specifications, geographic distribution and possible historical/publicity effects (pp.20–22). This is richer than reporting overlap alone, but the causal explanations are speculative. The source populations and dates differ, including a listed coaster absent from the operating dataset. Claims of superior accuracy need an external criterion, not just agreement with the model's own preferences. Proposed A/B testing of the app is a plan, not a completed experiment (p.27).

## Static manuscript and code checks

P.15 says length is discarded, yet its final indicator diagram and later sensitivity/radar analysis retain length. The prose says four PCA components will simplify the calculation, but the code computes `res` then scores the original normalized matrix `X` (pp.48–49). The code also swaps entropy weights across columns without a matching documented justification (p.49). These observations require tracing the actual executed pipeline before trusting the final feature set.

The supplied neural-network script uses five inputs including year, while the prose lists four; it calls 1000 training iterations, whereas the figure/discussion uses 100,000 (pp.11, 44–45). It separates missing rows from observed training rows, but does not implement the described held-out validation split in the displayed routine. It needs external spreadsheet files that are not embedded as complete data tables. These are reproducibility gaps, not a reproduced failure verdict.

The app section calls its filtering method AHP even though the preceding model is described as fuzzy evaluation plus entropy (p.25). The aggregate-diversity equation repeats the per-user expression instead of displaying an average across users (p.28). An integration review should align terminology, equations and actual algorithm dependencies.

## Transfer to the workflow

Use an indicator-decision table with keep/drop reasons and a final schema checked against code. Distinguish genuine extremes from corrupt values before imputation. Require a named error metric, stored split and per-field validation for learned imputation. Let a separate reviewer verify that every advertised transformation affects the final result. For a conceptual app, specify necessary future data and a realistic cold-start path, show one complete user journey, and label evaluation proposals as proposals. Retain the useful comparison of objective rankings and personalized recommendations without claiming that either is universally correct.
