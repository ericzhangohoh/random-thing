## Demand map and architecture

The paper supplies city and airport information-collection plans (pp.6–9), household-finance, energy and waste models (pp.9–13), illustrative Concord/San Francisco results (pp.13–16), an airport adaptation (pp.16–18), revisions/generalization (p.19), limitations (p.20), and the newspaper article (p.21). Appendix pp.22–24 derives bottled-water energy intensities; references occupy pp.25–27. It covers the requested deliverable types, although the revised policy is discussed without a revised numerical application and the airport receives formulas without a worked case.

The architecture is `survey responses → pre/post beverage shares and container choices → resource and financial changes → community interpretation`. Four drink sources—tap, large jugs, single-serving PET water and soda—carry different unit costs, energy requirements and packaging masses. Reusable-bottle purchases and drinking fountains are separate additions. This exposes the substitution mechanism rather than treating removal of PET sales as equal to elimination of all associated impacts.

The summary (p.1) argues that immediate costs/energy can rise while longer-run effects improve. The first proposition follows from the illustrative assumptions; the second is a qualitative scenario rather than an estimated time path. No dynamic replacement/adoption model or multi-year cash flow demonstrates the claimed transition.

## Why the authors choose these methods

The authors explicitly prioritize individual cost because it may influence public acceptance, and energy because it is measurable and data are more available than for broader emissions (pp.1, 9–10). Waste is kept as a distinct physical outcome. They decline to collapse these dimensions into one score because communities value them differently (p.20). That restraint is useful: a multidimensional policy comparison need not invent universal weights.

**Reviewer inference:** additive accounting is appropriate for answering which substitutions drive a result, provided every branch uses consistent units, boundaries and periods. Its simplicity does not remove the need to model behavior; the assumed post-ban drink mix is the largest causal input. The paper does not estimate this response from a realized policy change.

The airport objective changes from consumer cost to airport/concession revenue because temporary travelers do not face the city's household-tax setting (pp.16–18). The different decision-maker is explicitly motivated. Nevertheless, a government comparing welfare cannot silently interpret concession revenue as equivalent to household welfare or total social benefit.

## Data acquisition and variable interfaces

The strongest methodological contribution is the survey-to-variable derivation table (pp.6–9). The proposed city survey asks pre/post drink-source proportions, recycling behavior, brand choice, reusable-bottle ownership/intention, perceived tap-water safety and purchase motivations. A census is proposed if feasible; otherwise the authors suggest stratifying by age, gender and income. At airports they propose flight-cluster sampling and comparing intended alternatives with actual purchases. Brand frequencies are intended to estimate shipping distance.

This makes the research plan actionable, which matters because the official problem requires identifying data and collection methods without requiring collection. It also exposes repairable interface issues. The soda mapping points to the jug response, and some post-ban mappings reuse pre-ban question numbers. Drink shares should permit zero and sum to one. An unweighted mean of respondents' drink proportions does not necessarily equal the population's volume-weighted share. Self-reported recycling frequency is not the mass recovery rate, and stated post-ban intentions are not observed behavior. Flight-cluster estimates need passenger/flight selection weights and uncertainty. These are specific improvements to an otherwise useful plan, not evidence that a real survey was performed.

Illustrative city inputs include 14 L per person per week, 0.5 L PET bottles of 9.89 g, five-gallon jugs, $30 reusable bottles, and fountain costs (pp.4–6, 9–14). Concord uses 19,000 residents and 22→27 fountains; San Francisco uses 885,000 and 80→90. Both receive identical drink shares: PET 0.13→0, tap 0.67→0.73, jugs 0.12→0.14, soda 0.08→0.13, reusable ownership 0.45→0.48. The shares sum to one, but the common behavioral response is assumed. The 13% baseline is motivated by the proportion of people using bottled water as their primary source, which is not the same statistic as 13% of consumed volume.

Full compliance, negligible imported/alternative packaging and no manufacturer shift to slightly larger bottles simplify the city model. These assumptions require scenario tests. San Francisco's city-property restriction is not equivalent to elimination of all city PET purchases. The paper alternates between “less than” and “at most” one litre; the supplied policy includes the one-litre boundary. Large jugs are sometimes described with an inconsistent gallon-to-litre value, although the equations generally use 18.93 L.

## Equation and accounting audit

**Finance (pp.9–10, 14–15).** The drink-cost expression multiplies all share changes by the initial PET share. Yet those changes are defined as fractions of total fluids, not conditional fractions of displaced PET volume. This counts the baseline fraction twice unless the variables are redefined. Weekly drink spending is then added to a one-time reusable-bottle purchase and a mixture of fountain capital and annual maintenance. Reported $2.51/$1.42 effects are described as annual without an explicit conversion or amortization. Employment fraction 0.597 is used to approximate taxpayers, which is an incidence assumption, not a tax model. Ignoring already-funded fountains may describe new cash levies, but does not remove their resource/opportunity cost.

**Energy (pp.10–12).** Additive branches are understandable, but the prose gives 16.94 kWh per reusable bottle while its equation uses 24.47. Five-gallon jug production is charged on each use; the authors explicitly omit reuse because its frequency is uncertain (p.14). This uncertain parameter should instead be varied, especially because recommending jug reuse is a central proposed remedy. The tap-energy dimensional derivation is inconsistent with the printed coefficient even though the coefficient can be obtained from the earlier water-treatment assumption.

**Waste (pp.12–15).** The same initial-PET-share multiplication appears despite total-fluid share definitions. Recycling is alternately treated as a fraction and a percent. A common recovery rate is imposed across container types; incineration is defined but does not enter the final equation. Thus unrecycled packaging is effectively equated with landfill waste. The numerical line uses soda increment 0.5 rather than the stated 0.05, and the displayed San Francisco result repeats Concord's −18.003 kg while the prose gives −838.561 kg. These cannot all be accepted as a single verified calculation.

**City results (p.14).** The six displayed San Francisco energy terms sum to roughly 97,854 kWh, whereas the reported total is 97,853,884 kWh. Moreover, the surrounding section switches from weekly derivations to a one-year interpretation. The positive sign is therefore a conditional scenario result with reporting errors, not a validated measurement of the ban's impact.

**Energy appendix (pp.22–24).** Detailed derivations are valuable because they make boundary problems visible. Treatment costs add together alternative technologies, including seawater desalination, without process or market-share weights. The bottling equation evaluates to about 0.00259 kWh/L, ten times the printed 0.000259. Water collection is derived but omitted from the final sum. Truck energy includes electricity used to refine fuel rather than the fuel's combustion energy, while rail/ship coefficients represent transport energy; these branches do not share a consistent energy boundary. A 33% thermal-to-electric conversion is applied to some branches but not others, requiring an explicit primary/final-energy convention. Fiji/Evian distances determine the rail/ship split for trips beyond a local threshold even though imported water was assumed negligible. The appendix supports auditability, not confidence in the final intensity without repair.

## Airport, revision and validation limits

The airport adaptation correctly identifies security restrictions, short stays and different replacement options (pp.16–18). Its monthly revenue equation removes prior water revenue and adds reusable, soda and alternative-package revenue. However, replacement products' costs of goods are absent, so “net revenue” is not a complete profit calculation. Fountain capital cost is subtracted from monthly revenue without a horizon; $1,100 and $2,300 appear inconsistently. Fountain density is extrapolated using total airport land area, including areas irrelevant to passenger walking access, and produces a potentially negative or noninteger added count. A terminal-access or queue-capacity model would better represent service provision.

Policy proposals include tap-water communication, reusable/jug reuse, soda taxation to fund fountains, and broader PET restrictions (p.19). The authors identify which behavioral/material assumptions would change, but do not rerun the model. Their generalization discussion usefully recognizes cross-border purchasing, regional source-water differences and manufacturer adaptation. It does not establish that scaling population alone makes the model more accurate.

There is no empirical before/after validation, fitted behavioral model, sensitivity experiment or executable implementation. The problem allows a data-collection proposal, so absence of collected data is not itself noncompliance. What remains unsupported is the strength of policy conclusions beyond the assumed scenario. Health, water footprint and pollution are discussed qualitatively (pp.15–16), with some uncertainty acknowledged; they should not be presented as quantified model outputs.

## Transfer to the six-role workflow

The **coordinator** should preserve the exact policy scope and decision-maker for each case. The **domain-data** role can adopt this paper's question-to-variable table, adding units, population, sampling weights and observed-versus-intended status. The **modeler** should declare whether each share is unconditional or conditional and specify a common accounting boundary. The **computation** role should enforce share conservation, period conversion, component sums and integer nonnegative infrastructure counts. The **independent-reviewer** should audit changes of denominator, unit-energy basis and cost horizon before interpreting signs. The **writer-visuals** role should retain the paper's separate physical/financial outcomes and clear public article while distinguishing an illustrative scenario from an estimated causal effect.

The transferable strength is a visible chain from information needs to model inputs. The complementary lesson is that a detailed derivation still needs an independent audit of units, boundaries, definitions and arithmetic.
