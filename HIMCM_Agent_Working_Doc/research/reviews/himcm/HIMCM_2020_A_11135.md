## Decision structure and positive contributions

The paper separates two decisions: identifying a suitable job category and choosing among specific offers. Both applications use `qualification and unacceptable-preference filtering → personalized attribute indices → AHP-weighted benefits → benefits divided by normalized weekly hours → ranked recommendations` (pp.4–15). The category model uses wage, flexibility, social interaction and physical activity. The offer model uses wage, flexibility, commute, work environment and interest.

This separation of decision granularity is useful: national occupational attributes may guide exploration, while actual offers require local hours, pay, location and qualifications. The first-stage decision tree and explicit minimum acceptable wage/flexibility thresholds establish feasibility before compensatory scoring. The authors also recognize that social interaction and physical activity may have a preferred intermediate level, rather than being universally good or bad. Those are transferable modeling decisions even where their chosen transformations need revision.

The report supplies ten fictional profiles for both applications, preference matrices, output tables, a simple ranking comparator, sensitivity curves and a concrete app flow. The app includes an editable preference-weight screen so users can explore alternative rankings (pp.23–24). This connects sensitivity analysis to a decision the user can understand.

## Input meaning, data and feasibility

The category model starts from 44 occupational categories, but the full 44-row dataset and complete eligibility mapping are not supplied. Its sources include job guides, wage pages and methodological references; listing-level dates, wage definitions and rating provenance are insufficient for independent reconstruction (pp.9–11, 25). The specific-offer example provides five fictional offers, three candidate offers per person, and personalized weekly wages and commute times (pp.19–20). This is demonstration data, not verified hiring availability.

“Net weekly wage” is the declared variable, yet the category calculation describes median hourly wages and minimum hourly thresholds (pp.5, 10, 17). The offer table uses weekly amounts. These are different quantities, especially when the final score is divided by weekly hours. Define gross rate, actual paid hours, incremental expenses and net earnings separately, and keep their units through both applications. A minimum hourly wage does not guarantee a student's savings goal.

The paper assumes that students enter legal work hours and self-identify relevant qualifications. That is a stated modeling scope, not an implemented universal eligibility certificate. Its first home/office split also treats a binary preference as mandatory and does not show a neutral or flexible response. Users who can accept both should not be forced into an unnecessary exclusion. Physical limitations, unsafe work and unavailable schedules need hard limits even when a preferred range is used for otherwise feasible alternatives.

The pandemic assumption is a historical scenario about summer 2021, supported by optimism about vaccine development (p.3). It is not a verified prediction or a current health claim. Scenario-dependent assumptions should be revisitable rather than disappearing from the decision logic.

## Transformations: behavior must survive the mathematics

Wage and flexibility are transformed with Box–Cox, standardized, then min-max scaled for the category application (pp.10–11). A weighted decision score does not require normally distributed attributes, and standardization is defined for non-normal data. The report's transformed histogram is not a certificate that data are Gaussian. The relevant question is whether the resulting utility spacing represents a student's tradeoffs. The transformation parameter and fitting rule are not supplied. Min-max scaling after z-standardization is algebraically the same as min-max scaling the already transformed values, so that intermediate standardization adds no effect when variance is positive.

For social interaction and activity, users supply a preferred interval. The midpoint becomes a Gaussian mean and the interval width determines a standard deviation. The final index, however, is min-max scaling of negative absolute z-distance, not the Gaussian density (pp.11–12). This matters: with fixed midpoint and candidate set, `|z_i| = |x_i - mu| / sigma`, and the common `1/sigma` cancels from both numerator and denominator. Two users with the same preferred midpoint but different tolerance widths therefore receive identical final indices under the printed formula. The feature intended to encode tolerance does not survive the transformation. This is an algebraic finding, not numerical reproduction.

A useful repair would define a utility curve whose tolerance parameter remains active, or a piecewise acceptable interval with an explicit outside penalty, then test narrow versus broad intervals around the same center. The cited inverted-U relationship between arousal and performance motivates a possible shape but does not establish a normal probability distribution for preference over these ordinal job ratings. Preference utility, population distribution and uncertainty are separate objects.

Min-max denominators vanish when there is only one surviving job or when all surviving values are equal. The paper reports one-option cases for two students without specifying fallback normalization (p.17). State whether normalization uses the entire original pool or the feasible subset, and show how singleton, constant-column and empty-set cases are handled. A zero-width preferred interval and zero weekly hours also need explicit input behavior.

## Benefit per hour and flexibility

The final ratio divides a dimensionless weighted benefit by normalized hours (pp.8–9, 12, 15). This is an efficiency preference, not an automatically objective cost-benefit analysis or a calculation of money saved. Adding a constant to every job's benefit can change the ratio ranking when their hours differ; therefore arbitrary zero points of normalized utility matter. Very short jobs may score highly while yielding insufficient total earnings or learning. Compare this ratio with maximizing total benefit subject to a time budget and with an explicit income constraint.

The offer-level flexibility index multiplies remote-work share, shift availability and fewer workdays (pp.13–14). The rationale for rewarding balanced flexibility is understandable, but its calibration needs care. The text says a standard one-shift office job gets a shift factor of one; the printed `1 + shifts/3` gives more than one when shifts equals one. The sample assigns some jobs zero shifts despite positive hours. Define whether shifts means all possible shifts or additional choices beyond a fixed shift. The boundedness rationale also assumes particular limits on workdays and shifts that should be enforced. These details affect the meaning of the score, not just notation.

## Elicitation, comparison and evidence quality

The consistency procedure identifies a discrepant pairwise judgment and asks the user to reconsider it, rather than silently overwriting their preferences (p.8). This is a good interaction pattern. A low consistency ratio is an internal agreement diagnostic, not proof that preferences are accurate or that recommendations match real outcomes. Log original and revised answers, use a stopping rule and preserve the user's intended tradeoffs. The report provides comparison inputs but not all resulting consistency diagnostics. Brody's offer-level environment/interest comparison row is missing from the p.20 table, even though his results are reported.

The comparator is lexicographic ranking: sort by the most important criterion, then use lower priorities only for ties (pp.18–19). It is valuable to compare an alternative. Different rankings alone do not show that one is empirically more accurate, however. Lexicographic preferences can be legitimate when priorities are non-compensatory. The comparison also maximizes social interaction, whereas the proposed model evaluates distance from a preferred midpoint. A fair ablation should hold feasibility, attribute utility and objectives fixed while changing only the aggregation rule. Add a direct weighted-sum baseline to assess whether the AHP elicitation and transformations improve decisions.

The fictional biographies use social stereotypes and narrative predictions about how jobs will change personalities. Those descriptions are not measured skill or outcome evidence. Several explanations introduce capabilities and preferences that were not clearly established in the initial profiles. Canonical fixtures should state skills, constraints and utility inputs directly; qualitative plausibility checks should not be retrofitted to whichever occupation wins. The authors acknowledge that testing on actual users is future work (p.25), so “quantitatively accurate” exceeds the evidence from illustrative cases.

## Sensitivity and workflow transfer

The strongest validation element is the rank-change analysis for Arthur (pp.21–22). One weight varies while the ratios of the remaining weights are preserved. The authors report that tutoring remains first across the wage, flexibility and activity sweeps, while lawn care becomes first when the social-interaction weight reaches 0.498. This identifies a decision boundary rather than merely reporting a percentage change in an intermediate score. Preserve that method, but label its scope: one fictional profile, fixed data, fixed transformations and one-at-a-time weight changes. It does not test the tolerance-width failure, simultaneous changes or uncertain eligibility/data.

The **coordinator** should separate category exploration from offer selection and maintain complete canonical profiles. The **domain-data** role should distinguish hourly rates from weekly net earnings and document eligibility mappings. The **modeler** should confirm that each advertised preference parameter actually affects utility, and justify efficiency versus total-benefit objectives. The **computation** role should implement degenerate-input handling and retain transformation parameters. The **independent-reviewer** should use algebraic simplification, fixed-objective baselines and rank-change thresholds. The **writer-visuals** role should preserve the user-visible sensitivity screen and explain what a reported threshold does—and does not—establish.
