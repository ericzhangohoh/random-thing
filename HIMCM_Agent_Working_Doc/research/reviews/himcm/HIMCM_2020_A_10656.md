## Architecture and task coverage

The authors build a direct personalized scoring rule for paid jobs, then adapt it to unpaid internships. Their database contains 36 paid-job categories and 25 internship categories; ten fictional paid-job seekers and seven internship seekers demonstrate the outputs (pp.6–13, 21–24). The paid score multiplies hard eligibility gates for contact risk, physical demand, age and available work hours by soft rewards/penalties for wages, interests, lower contact risk and offered hours. A questionnaire feeds the model and a ranked shortlist preserves some user choice.

This is a useful alternative to selecting a named multi-criteria method first. Each term has a stated behavioral purpose. The authors explain why violating a non-negotiable condition should make a score zero, while falling below a preferred wage merely reduces it (pp.7–10). This makes assumptions open to challenge and yields simple testable invariants. The model is economical enough to implement and explain within the paper, and both code and full example data are supplied.

The paid-job task is covered with factors, ten fictional students, results and a presentation prototype. Internships are an extension rather than an official requirement. Earnings are represented by hourly-wage brackets, without calculating summer savings or total income. Hours are ordinal brackets, not a complete summer calendar. The prompt's requested classifications of constant/variable and deterministic/probabilistic factors are not developed into a systematic table or uncertainty model.

## Data scope and assumptions

The authors explain how they narrowed occupational data to jobs requiring no formal education, split broad occupations and selected a diverse subset. They cite OOH, O*NET, other wage sources and historical contact-risk guidance (pp.3–7, 17). This gives the dataset an understandable construction path. Nonetheless, “no formal education required” does not imply that every employer ignores experience, skills, credentials or training. The paper explicitly makes that stronger assumption and later identifies qualifications as future work.

The population is restricted to US citizens aged fourteen or above residing in the US. The citizenship restriction is not derived from the occupational data. National occupation categories are assumed to share wages, tasks and schedules. These are exploratory categories, not verified local vacancies. The authors exclude state/local employment rules from their stated scope; this review makes no claim about current legal eligibility. A deployed recommendation would need the actual location, applicable requirements and offer-specific checks. Commute is deferred to future implementation (p.17).

Risk labels reverse direction in one paragraph on p.7, while its concrete examples and the rest of the model assign remote work the lowest value. A data dictionary must fix direction once. The authors also narrow a historical occupational-risk classification to suit the sampled jobs. Those adapted ordinal categories are not measured infection probabilities. The paper's claims concern its historical scenario, not current public-health guidance.

## Scoring logic and boundary behavior

The soft components are understandable: a wage shortfall loses a factor of two per bracket; a gap between desired and offered hours loses a factor of 0.7 per bracket; shared interests are rewarded much more than shared non-interests; a binary preference doubles the exponent on either wages or interests. The authors acknowledge that these constants are somewhat arbitrary (p.16). Their rationale describes intended behavior but does not calibrate actual user tradeoffs. Perturb those constants as model assumptions, in addition to perturbing user answers.

A declared minimum acceptable wage is treated as a preference rather than a constraint. The questionnaire should distinguish a desired wage from a non-negotiable earnings floor. Likewise, maximum hours and desired hours are different: penalizing an offer because it uses fewer hours than the user's maximum can conflict with a desire for recreation. This needs separate willingness, preference and required-income inputs.

The printed hours gate on p.9 uses `Hm - Hi + 0.5` inside the sign expression. At the boundary `Hi = Hm`, that expression makes the gate zero, contrary to the prose statement that only `Hi < Hm` is excluded. The supplied code instead uses `hm - H - 0.5`, admitting equality (p.18). The equation and implementation therefore disagree at a consequential boundary. The subsequent “if and only if” list of reasons for a zero paid-job score omits the hours gate altogether (p.10). Simple explicit Boolean predicates would be clearer than complicated sign constructions for integer categories.

For internships, the interest factor can itself be zero when there are no shared positive or negative interest bits. Thus the claim that an internship score is zero only when risk or physical limits are exceeded is too strong (p.10). Conversely, a student with no positive interests receives nonzero scores through shared absences; the included all-zero-interest profile is a useful edge case but its top result should be presented as a fallback, not a validated expression of preference. Internship time and age constraints are removed rather than demonstrated unnecessary for the actual opportunities.

## Static code and data-contract audit

The Python listings are particularly valuable because they make the method inspectable (pp.17–20). The scoring function has named concepts, but its caller uses numeric CSV positions. In the printed job table, the schema is `job, age, risk, wage, physical demand, min hours, max hours, interests` (p.23). The call supplies `job[2]` not only as risk but also as wage and minimum age (p.18). With that published schema, the same risk value is therefore reused for three different concepts. The intended wage and age positions are different. This is a static interface mismatch; no claim is made here that the printed rankings were produced by this exact listing.

The result writer filters nonpositive scores before saving the shortlist, which is a useful safeguard when fewer than ten options are feasible. The console output prints the unfiltered top ten, so downstream interfaces must choose the filtered result and explain an empty shortlist. The displayed questionnaire omits the paid model's binary experience-versus-pay preference even though the scoring code and fictional-data table require it (pp.14–15, 18, 23). An input-to-variable map would reveal that omission before the writer and computation agents finalize separate artifacts.

There are small cross-artifact discrepancies as well: Person A is seventeen in the narrative but sixteen in the tables. The best-internship summary gives only IT support for Person L, whereas the appendix shows a three-way numerical tie. These observations motivate generated tables and one canonical profile record rather than manually duplicated values.

## Validation and presentation

The authors deliberately include moderate and extreme profiles, provide full ranked lists and report two interpretable perturbations: increasing available hours broadens suitable jobs, and increasing desired pay changes the leading options (pp.11–13). These are useful behavioral demonstrations. They do not establish the summary's claim that the profiles completely represent all job seekers, nor the conclusion that adding more jobs can only make matches more accurate. More alternatives can also add bad data or amplify model errors.

Radar charts compare selected job attributes with user inputs (pp.12–13). Similar shapes are not independent validation because the algorithm itself selects using those inputs, and different dimensions include maximum tolerances, minimum desires and ordinal categories. A lower risk than the user's tolerated maximum is beneficial even when it reduces visual overlap. Report criterion-by-criterion constraint satisfaction and tradeoffs rather than treating polygon similarity as accuracy.

The prototype is readable and ties question choices to model categories (p.15). It is explicitly a proposed implementation; the conclusion describes full implementation as future work. Preserve that honest distinction. The strengths/weaknesses table usefully admits missing commute, qualifications, schedule variation and arbitrary constants (p.16), though the generality claims elsewhere need the same restraint.

## Transfer to the six-role workflow

The **coordinator** should prioritize official tasks before extensions and maintain an interface-to-variable ledger. The **domain-data** role should preserve schema names, category meanings and eligibility scope. The **modeler** should separate hard limits from preferred targets and write boundary predicates explicitly. The **computation** role should use named columns, schema validation and boundary tests for equality, no feasible jobs and missing inputs. The **independent-reviewer** should reconcile equations, code, appendix data and displayed recommendations; a plausible narrative cannot substitute for that check. The **writer-visuals** role should generate results from canonical records and explain why an option meets constraints and trades off preferences, using charts as explanation rather than proof.
