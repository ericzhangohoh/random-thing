## Architecture and useful modeling choices

The pipeline combines five personalized indices—net salary, time, knowledge, environment and intensity—with AHP weights, TOPSIS ranking and hierarchical clustering. It then recommends the highest-ranked option from each cluster to increase variety (pp.4–17). A detailed example precedes nine additional preference profiles, and the paper provides a survey and several app layouts.

The strongest modeling contribution is the explicit construction of the indices. Net salary subtracts work-related transport and meal costs; time includes commute, recreation and conflicts with an existing schedule; overtime affects both expected earnings and available time; learning combines personal relevance, academic content, experience and social skills (pp.4–6). These definitions link model quantities to the student's decision more closely than simply rating every job with one generic number. The separate learning subweights show how two students with the same broad learning priority can still prefer different kinds of growth.

Clustering is used for diversity across attributes rather than merely dividing a scalar score into bands. This is a meaningful additional objective, provided the model states how much suitability it may sacrifice for diversity. The authors explicitly identify a weak option retained to ensure diversity in the sensitivity example (p.17), making that tradeoff visible.

## Requirements and feasibility

The report covers a factor system, personalized evaluation, ten fictional profiles and a presentation design. Its restatement says that developing an app and making the ten profiles globally representative are requirements; the official prompt requires a proposed presentation layout and reasonable fictional examples, not those stronger claims (p.1). A requirements ledger should distinguish the contest demand from an extension chosen by the team.

All jobs are assumed lawful and suitable for high-school students, and experience requirements are delegated to the student (p.2). The appendix nevertheless includes a PhD research internship, senior analyst, specialist clinical/technical roles and other titles whose eligibility cannot be assumed from an attractive score (pp.23–24). The authors acknowledge that repeatedly recommended roles require specific experience or knowledge (p.17). Feasibility screening must precede preference ranking; returning an attractive but inaccessible occupation does not solve a student's job-selection problem.

The model fixes daily work plus recreation availability at twelve hours, but allows salary or other benefits to compensate for schedule conflict and negative free time. The appendix's remote technical-writer entry is assigned start/end times of 0 and 24, and the body explicitly treats that as a twenty-four-hour work requirement (p.13). Whether that field represents availability or required labor is crucial. An invalid schedule should trigger a data/feasibility check rather than simply receive a low time index. A minimum savings target or non-negotiable commitment needs a hard constraint distinct from a soft preference.

## Index definitions and uncertainty

The net-benefit idea is strong, but its accounting needs precise units and baselines (p.5). `Dtravel` is described as distance between home and workplace, while transport cost and travel time each multiply it once; specify one-way versus daily round-trip distance. Subtract incremental meal costs attributable to working, rather than automatically all three daily meals if a job supplies none. A student must eat even without the job. Fixed transport charges and modes also need a stated approximation.

Expected overtime earnings are probability times duration times overtime rate; expected overtime duration also enters the time score. This is a coherent first-order expectation calculation if the conditional duration/rate and probability are defined consistently. It does not show whether an occasional late shift breaches a hard commitment. The expected value can conceal an unacceptable tail outcome. A scenario or chance-of-conflict check would address that question without requiring an unnecessarily complex full stochastic model.

Learning is assumed proportional to work hours, with qualitative gains scored one to five and academic relevance switched on or off (pp.5–6). That is an explicit approximation, but longer hours do not automatically yield proportionately more useful learning. Check saturation and whether hours are rewarded in learning while already penalized in time. Environment and intensity ratings also encode values; an objective computation does not make those judgments universally objective. The intensity preference favors less laborious work, which may not suit every student's preferences.

## AHP, TOPSIS and clustering specification

The paper lays out the mathematical steps for all three methods, supplies a worked top-level comparison matrix and two submatrices, and reports the corresponding weights (pp.6–12). This is helpful for auditability. However, descriptions of generic method advantages do not establish that this particular application improves on a simpler weighted score. A baseline comparison should evaluate the resulting decisions, not the number of methods used.

Equation 7.13 prints an eigenvalue formula with an extra subtraction by the matrix dimension before division by one weight. That does not follow from the preceding eigenvector equation. The usual eigenvalue relationship and the consistency-index subtraction are separate operations. The report gives consistency formulas but does not report a complete set of consistency results for the ten profiles. Numerical reproduction would need the actual matrices, the chosen diagnostic threshold and executable computation.

TOPSIS distances depend on normalized criteria and the candidate set. Check zero-norm criteria, identical alternatives, near ties and changes when an irrelevant offer is added. The clustering section specifies Euclidean point distance and iterative merging, but leaves the inter-cluster linkage update and the exact scaling of clustering inputs unclear (pp.10–11). The example cuts a dendrogram at height 25 to form eight groups without a decision-based justification (p.13). Clustering raw dollars, hours and ratings can be driven by units; clustering normalized indices can answer a different question. Both the inputs and threshold require an explicit contract.

Choosing one representative per cluster can elevate low-value offers. The method should enforce eligibility and a minimum utility or maximum-regret tolerance before selecting diverse representatives. “Different” is useful only within a set the student can reasonably accept. Report the tradeoff against a plain top-k shortlist and test sensitivity to linkage, scaling and cut height.

## Data provenance, demonstrations and sensitivity

The appendix is titled “Fictional Job Data” and lists 31 offers with salaries, start/end times, overtime assumptions, meals, remote flags, coordinates and qualitative attributes (pp.22–24). The body separately assumes that its data are real and reliable. The status of each column—observed, synthesized or rated—must be stated. No listing-level source/date supports all these fields. Coordinates clustered near San Francisco are paired with some job titles naming other locations, consistent with an illustrative dataset rather than verified vacancies. Salary currency and the meanings of the compact `T` and `S` appendix headings also need explicit units.

The ten people mainly express one dominant inclination each, and full schedules, locations, matrices and preference coefficients are not supplied for all of them (pp.14–16). They demonstrate contrasting preferences, not representation of students around the world. Although an online survey is proposed, a respondent sample and survey results are not reported. The detailed worked example is useful, while nine short preference labels cannot substitute for a complete reproducible test fixture.

Changing the worked student's main priority from knowledge to recreation changes the recommendations (pp.17–18). That demonstrates responsiveness to a substantial preference change. It does not demonstrate stability to small measurement errors, and the paper's statement that high sensitivity ensures stability conflates two different properties. Test desired responsiveness to meaningful preference changes separately from undesired sensitivity to uncertain data or weakly supported assumptions.

## Interface and workflow transfer

The app mockups and six-question survey make the user-facing concept concrete (pp.18–21). Yet the interface asks for ranking four broad factors while the model requires five top-level weights, subcriterion judgments, schedule details and other inputs. A rank order is not itself a complete AHP pairwise matrix. Gender and school are requested without an explicit mathematical role. Each required input should map to a variable, and each collected field should have a justified purpose. The reported artifact supports a design proposal, not a completed end-to-end app.

The **coordinator** should distinguish required deliverables from extensions and define the acceptable cost of diversity. The **domain-data** role should label fictional versus observed fields, verify units and screen eligibility. The **modeler** should preserve the useful net-benefit and conflict logic while separating hard constraints, expectations and preferences. The **computation** role should publish complete profile fixtures, diagnostic values and clustering settings. The **independent-reviewer** should test candidate-set effects, infeasible schedules and diversity-induced regret. The **writer-visuals** role should keep the worked example and explicit mockups, use stable job IDs across zero-/one-based indexing, and distinguish behavioral sensitivity from robustness.
