## Task response and model architecture

The paired official 2017 A problem requires three displays, drone counts and locations, mathematical animated paths, show-level requirements and a mayoral memo. The paper builds a reusable pipeline rather than three disconnected models:

`Binary image → boundary extraction → corner-aware point selection → frames → bottleneck bipartite matching between frames → constant-speed trajectories → rotation/shear animation → launch/landing schedule → fleet, space and duration.`

The regular Ferris wheel is generated directly; irregular dragon and eagle outlines are sampled from images. A scene contains drone coordinates, and transformations act on selected subsets. The shared fleet has 480 drones, with two dark standby points in the 478-point dragon (p.25). The two-page memo translates the geometry and timing into ground clearance, airspace and feasibility claims (pp.2–3).

## Explicit approach-selection reasoning

The strongest reasoning appears before the algorithm. A transition finishes when the last drone reaches its target. With a common constant speed, completion time depends on the **largest assigned travel distance**, not the total distance. This motivates a bottleneck perfect matching between the old and new point sets (pp.12–13). Thresholding edges converts the optimization into a sequence of matching-feasibility questions; binary search narrows the admissible maximum distance (pp.13–14). This is an unusually clear chain from operational definition to objective, mathematical structure and solver.

Contour extraction alone supplies too many pixels. The authors therefore select approximately spaced boundary points, then preserve corners by detecting a reversal in distance from the previous selected point. A paired figure shows the difference in eagle detail (pp.11–12). Rotation describes the wheel; shear describes dragon motion; a pair of off-origin wing rotations describes the eagle (pp.15–19). The eagle motivates homogeneous coordinates: translate the wing's axis to the origin, rotate, then translate back. These are task-driven additions to a common transformation framework.

**Reviewer inference:** this paper is a strong example for the model-selection gate: identify what determines the stakeholder's outcome before choosing an objective. However, bottleneck assignment is an optimal assignment model under the simplified geometry; it is not automatically a minimum-time collision-free trajectory planner.

## Data, assumptions and parameter interfaces

Equipment specifications and historical show examples supply speed, battery duration, physical size and separation assumptions (pp.7–8). The model adopts 3 m/s constant speed, a 1.5 m separation reference and an audience buffer based on previous events. It assumes that onboard technology handles obstacles and collisions, simplifying most paths to straight lines (p.8). This transfers a central feasibility obligation outside the mathematical optimization.

Image coordinates are scaled to a nominal 120 m by 120 m display with its base above the ground (p.25). Parameters connect image resolution to fleet count, model coordinates to physical size, travel distance to time, and display bounds to audience clearance. Launch planning projects target positions to the ground and staggers departure so different-height drones arrive together (pp.19–20). The lower-bound argument is exact only for the ideal vertical geometry; multiple ground rows and horizontal offsets need their own timing and separation verification.

The memo acknowledges that rental prices are not known rather than inventing a quotation (p.3). Historical regulatory claims and a presumed waiver are not evidence of current permission or actual operational feasibility.

## Validation, sensitivity and supportable claims

The paper demonstrates visual resolution changes for sharp and smooth outlines (pp.22–24). This supports the claim that shape complexity affects the useful drone count. It does not identify an objectively optimal count without a defined fidelity metric and cost function. An illustrative assignment diagram suggests that fewer drones may increase the longest trip; this is not a general monotonicity theorem for differently sampled point sets (pp.23–24).

The main solution estimates 30 and 50 seconds for its two transitions and 69 seconds for launch plus landing; three 60-second displays yield 329 seconds (pp.25–26). The feasibility claim is principally a resource-envelope check. The concluding remarks appropriately identify GPS error, weather and collision-avoidance reliability as unresolved (pp.26–27). There is no displayed exhaustive continuous-time separation audit, acceleration model, uncertainty propagation or experimental validation.

## Static consistency and implementation checks

These observations come from reading and inspection, not running the code:

- The printed position expression before arrival on p.14 omits the initial-position offset, although the animation code adds it (p.41). A unit test at time zero would expose the manuscript/code mismatch.
- The refinement loops in the C++ listing increment a candidate variable but assign the threshold to `stf`, rather than that candidate, on each pass (p.39). Thus the displayed code does not substantiate the claimed decimal refinement as written. An end-to-end execution and comparison against a small exact matching example are needed.
- The memo gives the second transition as 59 seconds, while the main result uses 50 seconds; the 329-second total follows the latter (pp.3, 25–26). The battery limit is 20 minutes early in the paper but 1500 seconds in the conclusion (pp.7, 26).
- The animation appendix uses launch/landing segment durations of 40 and 63 seconds, and switches the third segment after 30 seconds despite constructing it with a 50-second duration (p.41). These configuration differences require reconciliation before a reproducibility claim.

## Transfer to the working document

Require the modeler to explain why the objective measures the actual completion criterion, as this paper does for the last-arriving drone. Require an explicit interface table for pixels, scaled coordinates, speed, fleet count and time. Assign a separate validator to check geometric feasibility throughout transitions; a solver's assignment feasibility is a different property. Require the implementation reviewer to test time-zero/end-point identities and compare printed pseudocode, executed code and final parameters. Finally, let the writer retain the paper's useful statement of unresolved physical risks while aligning the summary, memo, figures and appendix to one frozen run.
