## Demand map and architecture

The official task asks for slopes of varying lengths, at least 160 km in total, approximately 20/40/40 difficulty distribution, resort comparison and a client memo. The paper covers these through terrain processing (pp.6–12), binary network selection (pp.13–15), AHP-based resort scoring (pp.16–25), and the two-page memo/attachment (pp.3–4).

`Google Earth ridgeline and elevation data + Terrain2STL point cloud → local normal vectors → relatively level candidate sites → clustered sites and representative peaks → 13×13 possible-edge representation → binary route selection → designed-resort indicators → AHP evaluation against 15 other resorts.`

The key reusable architecture is the conversion of a continuous spatial problem into a finite decision set. Terrain processing makes the optimization tractable, but also defines which possible designs the optimizer can ever find. The second model evaluates the constructed alternative using the same attributes as its competitors.

## Approach and reported justification

The paper explains local normals geometrically: their angle to the upward vertical vector represents local steepness; relatively small angles identify smoother construction sites (pp.9–11). It reverses inward-facing normals before comparing them (p.10). K-means reduces candidate sites and peaks (pp.12–13). Binary variables encode whether potential links are selected. Total length is minimized as a proxy for construction expenditure while satisfying length and difficulty requirements (pp.13–15).

A separate MCDA structure distinguishes resort size, variety, convenience and snow quality (pp.16–21). Min-max normalization is justified as putting unlike units on a common scale; AHP is used to make pairwise preference comparisons explicit and to check their internal consistency (pp.17–19). The final score aggregates seven subcriteria. Tables show the comparison matrices, normalization, consistency measures and final weights (pp.22–24), making this part comparatively inspectable.

**What remains unreported:** why the particular number of clusters and representative peaks is sufficient, whether alternate discretizations change the selected design, and how stakeholder preferences informed the pairwise judgments. The generic prevalence of linear programming or AHP is not a problem-specific reason by itself. The practical argument for shorter construction is clear, but length alone is an uncalibrated expenditure proxy and may conflict with the desirability of long trails.

## Data and assumptions

The authors locate peaks from local elevation maxima and bowls from adjacent profiles (pp.6–8); the coordinate appendix supports geographic provenance (p.28). Resort information combines the supplied spreadsheet and external websites (pp.21–22). Assumptions allow construction throughout the ranch and assume accurate input data (p.6). These simplify the feasible set but do not establish construction suitability. An operational model would need terrain, environmental, capacity and access constraints or a narrower claim.

The binary model describes length proportions in prose but writes expressions based on counts of selected links (p.14). This matters: the denominator and unit define what is actually being constrained. A task-specific data dictionary should distinguish a link, a complete ski route, total unique built distance and traversed distance. Straight-line distances between nodes also need comparison with actual surface routes.

## Validation and supported conclusions

AHP consistency ratios below the stated threshold are reported and displayed (pp.23–24). This supports coherence of pairwise judgments, not accuracy of the judgments or external validity of the ranking. The results provide an inspectable route list with reported total 160.76556 km (pp.4, 15), and a final resort score of 0.49, sixth among the 16 listed alternatives (p.25). No parameter-sensitivity experiment, alternative discretization comparison, independent optimization certificate, or comparison with a simple feasible design is reported.

The limitations acknowledge oversimplified geometry and omission of transportation, location and safety in evaluation (p.26). The review supports describing the output as a proposed design conditional on those abstractions, not a demonstrated globally most cost-effective real-world resort.

**Static consistency issues to catch in the workflow:** the abstract says 21 routes and top five, the memo says 20 routes and sixth, and the displayed design table lists 19 routes while the final ranking is sixth (pp.1, 3–4, 15, 25). The network discussion selects three peaks, while the output includes Peak4 (pp.12–15). The length constraint says miles in one prose line while the task and result table use kilometers (p.14). Exact 20/40/40 constraints also need reconciliation with the displayed difficulty counts. No corrected optimization has been run here.

## Transfer to the working document

1. Require a representation justification before an algorithm justification: explain how continuous terrain becomes nodes and feasible edges.
2. Treat discretization as a modeling decision; test whether resolution or candidate-selection changes important conclusions.
3. A modeler-to-computation handoff must specify the unit and meaning of every constraint, especially counts versus lengths and links versus routes.
4. Show intermediate tables when they make judgments and transformations auditable. AHP consistency is one check, not a complete validation strategy.
5. Generate all reported counts, ranks and totals from a common result table to prevent abstract/body/memo divergence.
6. Separate the design objective from its evaluation metric, and explicitly discuss when they reward conflicting attributes.

The strongest transferable feature is the concrete chain from geographic data to a finite graph and then to an evaluative comparison. It provides role interfaces that can be inspected individually and at integration time.
