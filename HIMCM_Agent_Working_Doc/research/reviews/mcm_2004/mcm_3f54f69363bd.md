## Problem structure and strongest reusable architecture

The operational problem is to control a physical queue and a virtual-return queue for an amusement-park ride while preventing contradictory return-time promises and starvation of the ordinary queue. The paper decomposes it into **environment model → customer behavior → queue-state measurement → control policy → repeated simulation → comparison** (pp.4–14).

Its strongest reusable feature is an explicit baseline and incremental model ladder. The authors first expose a failure in a deliberately simple system, improve the environment and behavior models separately, then compare policies that adjust one control at a time and finally both. The result is an interpretable experimental story, rather than a collection of unrelated algorithms.

## Model architecture and cross-module links

1. Arrivals and groups: a Poisson arrival process and shifted-Poisson group size create demand. Constant arrival intensity is later replaced by a time-of-day function (pp.5–9).
2. Decisions: a 50/50 choice baseline becomes a rule depending on desire to ride, ordinary-queue length and virtual return time. This alters inflows to both queues (pp.7–8).
3. State estimation: recent inflows, outflows and queue populations estimate waiting times; short regressions smooth noisy rates and extrapolate near-future conditions (pp.5, 9–12).
4. Promise invariant: the controller retains an internal return-time state so subsequently issued passes cannot promise earlier return times than those already issued (p.7).
5. Controllers: adjust pass issuance with fixed boarding ratio; adjust boarding ratio with fixed pass issuance; then vary both (pp.9–12).
6. Optional extension: a third, reservation-like queue is designed but explicitly excluded from comparisons because it was not fully implemented (pp.13–14).
7. Experiment layer: individual-day trajectories diagnose mechanisms; 60-day runs summarize variability and compare four implemented policies (pp.15–32).

The demand/behavior model and controller are distinguishable components, which permits partial changes and ablations. Feedback is explicit: queue states affect customer choices and controller decisions, which then alter future queue states. Combining controllers introduces an initialization trap: a boarding ratio near one can prevent passes from being issued, leaving no QuickPass observations to trigger updates (p.12).

## Approach and justification

**Author reports:** stochastic arrivals and small-group distributions approximate how customers reach rides (p.5). Constant intensity and indiscriminate queue choice are intentionally simple baselines; their shortcomings motivate peak-hour arrivals and behavior-dependent choice (pp.7–9, 17–19). The boarding ratio directly addresses starvation caused by unconditional QuickPass priority. Normalized waiting-time objectives distinguish the intended service levels of the two queues (pp.9–12).

**Reviewer interpretation:** the algorithm is justified through the failure it fixes and the variable it can control. The internal-clock rule is a particularly clear example of converting a complaint into a testable invariant. The model ladder also separates added realism in the simulated environment from changes in the policy being evaluated.

Important limits: the arrival, desire, group-size and return distributions are proposed approximations, not fitted customer-behavior laws. The 20-minute and 2.5-hour waiting thresholds are design preferences, not empirically established optimal welfare weights. Polynomial rate prediction is a control component, not independently validated long-range forecasting.

## Validation design, results and boundaries

**Author-reported checks and findings:**

- Arrival rates and pass-allotment sizes are varied. Severe overload demonstrates how unconditional priority can nearly halt ordinary-queue outflow (pp.15–18).
- Behavior and time-varying arrivals are introduced separately and together, explaining which change creates which trajectory (pp.19–23).
- Boarding ratio and pass count are varied separately, then jointly. Time-series panels show flows, return-time promises, waits and control settings (pp.22–29).
- Four implemented models are compared over 60 simulated days using hourly waiting estimates, remaining customers, peak waits, aggregate wait measures and pass-taking fractions; means and standard deviations are reported (pp.29–32).
- The authors report no clear statistical separation of aggregate outcomes under their near-capacity default conditions, while acknowledging that overload may distinguish the policies more strongly (pp.30, 33).

**Evidence limits:** simulated comparisons demonstrate behavior within the assumed environment, not real-park performance. Mean ± standard deviation is reported; an explicit hypothesis test or uncertainty interval for a paired between-policy difference is not supplied. Therefore the claim of no statistically significant difference should be retained as an author claim, not independently certified. The workload regime is narrowed after extreme overload produces failures (p.17); this boundary must accompany recommendations.

The authors candidly acknowledge that flow-based wait estimates are noisy and do not directly measure each individual's waiting time. They propose tracking individuals to estimate actual waiting more accurately. No-shows, abandonment and interactions across several rides are absent (p.30).

## Communication and integrity

The abstract and executive summary distinguish the implemented four-policy experiment from the unimplemented third-queue concept (pp.1, 3, 13). The model chapters come before result chapters, with repeated plot arrangements that make variants comparable. The manuscript explains unexpected lack of aggregate improvement instead of simply declaring the most complex policy superior (pp.29–33).

Static review flags: notation in the return-time equation and subsequent procedural definition should be reconciled before reuse: p.7 initially combines a wait duration with a system time, while its explicit steps use current time plus waiting time. The diagram on p.10 also warrants an independent branch-condition check against its surrounding prose. These are audit targets, not a claim that the unexecuted implementation necessarily contains those errors.

## Reusable operating rules

1. Define an environment simulator separately from the intervention/controller.
2. State a concrete baseline, demonstrate its failure, and add one mechanism at a time.
3. Translate requirements into invariants, not only average metrics.
4. Test the initialization and feedback behavior of coupled modules.
5. Use repeated stochastic runs and compare uncertainty in differences, not only attractive single-run plots.
6. Test ordinary, peak and overload regimes; do not remove failures without stating the narrowed operating range.
7. Distinguish measured per-agent outcomes from proxy estimates used for control.
8. Preserve honest null results; added complexity needs evidence of value.
9. Label proposed-but-unimplemented extensions explicitly and exclude them from validated results.
